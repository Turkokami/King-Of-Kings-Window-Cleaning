## What is actually on your concrete

"Dirty" covers several different things, and they do not all come off the same
way. Knowing which you have is the difference between a satisfying result and a
disappointing one.

**Biological film** — the green, grey or black layer that makes a path look
tired and makes it slippery. Algae, with moss in the joints and lichen on older
surfaces. This is the bulk of what we remove and it responds well: a cleaning
solution kills the growth and the pressure lifts it off. On a shaded path this is
essentially the entire job.

**Embedded dirt and grit.** Ordinary soil worked into a porous surface over
years. Comes out with pressure and a surface cleaner, and this is where the
dramatic before-and-after line comes from.

**Oil and vehicle fluids.** A different problem entirely, because oil has soaked
*into* the slab rather than sitting on it. Fresh oil often lifts substantially
with a degreaser and heat. Old oil that has migrated deep into porous concrete
may lighten rather than vanish, and no amount of pressure changes that — the
staining is below the surface being cleaned. We will give you an honest read
before starting.

**Rust.** From metal furniture, rebar, fertiliser, or well water used in
irrigation. Rust is a chemical stain and it needs a rust-specific treatment;
pressure alone does nothing to it and can drive it deeper.

**Tannin staining.** Brown or reddish discolouration under and around trees,
particularly from conifer needles, bark mulch, and fallen leaves left in place
over winter. Very common here. It responds to the right chemistry and stubbornly
resists the wrong one.

**Efflorescence.** The chalky white bloom that appears on concrete, brick and
pavers as mineral salts migrate to the surface and crystallise. It looks like
someone spilled something. It is the material itself, and it needs a specific
treatment rather than force.

**Paint, sealer failure and tyre marks.** Each its own approach, and each worth
identifying before rather than during.

## Why the north wall is always the problem

Almost every house-washing call in this region is really a call about one
elevation, so it is worth explaining what is going on with it.

At this latitude the sun tracks low across the southern sky, and a north-facing
wall receives effectively no direct sun for months at a time. It is the last
surface on the property to dry after rain and the first to be damp again. In a
climate where the wet season runs from October into May, that wall is
functionally never dry.

Algae needs three things: moisture, a surface to sit on, and light — but far less
light than a plant does. A permanently damp north wall supplies all three, which
is why the growth is not a sign of neglect. It is a sign of aspect.

Several other factors stack on top of it:

**Overhanging trees and tall shrubs** extend the shaded, non-drying zone further
around the building, which is why growth often wraps around onto the east and
west elevations under the canopy line.

**Planting close to the wall** is the most common aggravating factor we see.
Shrubs planted against siding block airflow, hold humidity against the surface,
and splash soil onto the lower courses in heavy rain. A foot of clearance makes a
measurable difference; two is better.

**Prevailing weather.** Wind-driven rain comes predominantly from the south and
west here, so those elevations get wetter — but they also get sun, so they dry.
The north wall gets less rain and holds what it gets.

**Downspout and gutter discharge** landing on or splashing back onto a wall keeps
one patch permanently wet, and that patch will be visibly worse than the wall
around it.

**Sprinkler overspray** hitting siding does the same thing daily, and on hard
water it adds mineral spotting to the biological growth.

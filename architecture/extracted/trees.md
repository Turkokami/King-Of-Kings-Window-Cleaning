## Tree by tree — what is actually landing in your gutters

Which trees are near the house changes the schedule more than anything else, and
it is worth knowing what you have.

**Douglas fir** is the defining problem of gutter maintenance in this region.
Sheds needles continuously with a heavy autumn drop, and the needles mat into the
felt described above. Also drops cones, which are large enough to block an outlet
by themselves.

**Western red cedar** sheds in flat sprays rather than individual needles, and
those sprays knit into an even more effective mat than fir does. Cedar debris
also breaks down slowly, so it accumulates rather than composting away.

**Western hemlock** produces very fine needles and small cones — the fine needles
pass through almost any guard mesh and go straight into building the silt layer.

**Big-leaf maple**, common through the river valleys and older neighbourhoods,
drops leaves large enough to bridge a downspout outlet whole, plus an enormous
quantity of seeds in spring that germinate happily in gutter silt.

**Alder**, which colonises disturbed and damp ground throughout both counties,
drops small cones and catkins that behave like gravel in a gutter.

**Birch and ornamental cherry** in planted neighbourhoods contribute fine twiggy
debris and blossom drop, which is a spring problem rather than an autumn one and
catches people who only clean in November.

A house surrounded by lawn with no overhang can genuinely manage on one visit a
year. A house under mature Douglas fir needs four, and no product on the market
changes that.

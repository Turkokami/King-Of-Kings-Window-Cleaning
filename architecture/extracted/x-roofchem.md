## What is in the solution, and why we will tell you

Homeowners are entitled to know what is being put on their roof and running into
their garden, and the industry has not always been forthcoming about it.

Broadly there are two families of roof-cleaning chemistry.

**Chlorine-based solutions** — sodium hypochlorite, diluted, usually with a
surfactant. This is the standard approach for algae on asphalt and it is what the
asphalt roofing industry's own maintenance guidance describes: a dilute bleach
solution, gently applied, thoroughly rinsed. It kills the organism quickly and
the visual result is immediate. Its drawbacks are the ones you would expect —
it is hard on planting if runoff is not managed, it can lighten some surfaces,
and the smell is noticeable on the day.

**Non-chlorine alternatives** — typically based on zinc or potassium salts, or
on quaternary compounds. Gentler on planting and on some coatings, slower to
show a result, and generally more expensive per square foot. On cedar, on some
metal coatings, and in sensitive catchments, they are the right choice.

Which one goes on your roof depends on the roofing material, what is growing, and
where the runoff goes. That last one matters more here than in most places: in
the **Lake Whatcom watershed**, which supplies drinking water and covers Sudden
Valley, Geneva and the surrounding catchment, what leaves a roof is a real
consideration and it constrains the method.

Ask any roof-cleaning contractor what they are using and how they are handling
runoff. A straight answer is a good sign.

## Prevention — zinc and copper, and what they actually do

Once a roof is clean, the obvious question is how to keep it that way.

The established answer is **metal strips at the ridge**: zinc or copper, fitted so
that rain running over them carries a trace of dissolved metal ions down the roof
surface. Those ions are toxic to algae and moss at very low concentrations, which
is why you sometimes see a clean stripe running down an otherwise streaked roof
below a copper flashing or a galvanised vent. That stripe is the mechanism
demonstrating itself.

What is worth knowing about it:

- **It works best as prevention, not cure.** Strips slow recolonisation on a clean
  roof. They do very little to growth that is already established.
- **Coverage is limited by rainfall path.** The effect reaches only where the
  water actually runs, so a roof with multiple planes, dormers or wide runs needs
  more than one strip, and areas the water bypasses stay untreated.
- **The effect diminishes downslope.** Concentration drops as water travels, so
  the bottom of a long run gets less protection than the top.
- **Copper is more effective and more expensive than zinc**, and copper runoff
  onto some surfaces stains.

Strips are fitted by roofers, not by us. But it is worth raising if a roof is
being replaced or reroofed, because that is the cheap moment to do it.

The other prevention lever is **shade**. Overhanging branches keep a roof damp,
drop organic material onto it, and block the sun that would otherwise dry it.
Cutting back branches that overhang the roof does more for regrowth intervals
than any product, and it also reduces what ends up in the gutters.

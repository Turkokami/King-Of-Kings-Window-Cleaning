## The glass itself matters, and it varies by street

Ferndale housing does not come from one era, and the glass tells you which era
you are standing in front of.

**Older farmhouses and pre-war stock** often still carry single-pane annealed
glass, sometimes original, sometimes in original wooden sashes. This is the
softest, most forgiving glass to clean and the least forgiving frame to work
around. Old putty and old paint chip if you are careless with a blade, and the
sashes themselves are frequently painted partly shut. We work these slowly and we
do not force anything.

**Nineteen-seventies through nineteen-nineties subdivisions** are mostly
early double glazing in aluminium or early vinyl frames. Two things show up here
constantly. The first is failed seals — the cloudiness *between* the panes that
looks like a cleaning problem and is not one; no external cleaning reaches the
inside of a sealed unit, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling you a
clean that cannot work. The second is track wear, because twenty-odd years of
Ferndale field grit in a sliding track does exactly what you would expect to the
rollers and the weatherstripping.

**Newer construction** — and Ferndale has a great deal of it — brings big fixed
panes, more tempered glass in and around doors and low openings, and low-E
coatings. Low-E is the one to know about. The coating is usually on an interior
surface of the sealed unit and is not what you are touching, but the glass reads
differently in raking light and people frequently mistake the coating's own
faint sheen for a film that will not come off. Tempered glass is the other one:
it is stronger, but it carries microscopic surface debris from the tempering
process, and aggressive blade work on tempered glass can produce fine scratching
that was latent in the pane rather than caused by the dirt. It is a real
phenomenon, it is well documented in the trade, and it is why post-construction
glass gets quoted as its own job rather than folded into a normal clean.

None of this is trivia. It changes the method, it changes the time, and on a
Ferndale street where a 1978 rambler sits next to a 2019 build, it changes it
house to house.

## Access, ladders and why the licensing question matters

A meaningful share of Ferndale homes have at least one window that is genuinely
awkward — over a deck, above a slope, at the top of a stairwell, or set into a
gable a long way off the ground. This is the part of the job where the difference
between a licensed, insured operation and a cheap one stops being paperwork.

Falls are the real hazard in this trade, and they mostly happen when someone
improvises: a ladder footed on soft ground, a ladder leaned against a gutter, a
reach made from the top step because moving the ladder again felt like too much
work. The water-fed pole exists precisely so that a second-storey window can be
cleaned with both feet on the ground. Where the pole cannot reach and a ladder
genuinely cannot be set safely, the right answer is to say so, and we do.

King of Kings Window Cleaning LLC is a registered Washington contractor and
carries Labor & Industries coverage. Ask for the registration number and you will
get it before the work starts. It is worth asking anyone who quotes you — not as
a formality, but because an uninsured fall on your property is your problem in a
way most homeowners have never thought about.

## Why homeowners here call a company rather than doing it themselves

Plenty of people do clean their own windows, and on a single-storey house with
easy access and soft water, that is a reasonable decision. The reason it stops
being reasonable in Ferndale specifically comes down to three things.

The first is the water. Washing hard water onto glass and letting it dry in the
sun is how the spotting problem starts, and a bucket from the outside tap is
exactly that. Purified water is not a luxury item on this side of the county; it
is the thing that makes the result last.

The second is the film. Industrial haze and dried field dust do not respond to a
spray bottle and a paper towel — they smear, which is why so many people report
that their windows look worse after they clean them than before. Breaking bonded
film takes the right detergent and physical contact, and then a clean pull to
take it off rather than move it around.

The third is the reach. Most of the genuinely dirty glass on a Ferndale house is
the glass nobody wants to get to: the second storey, the stairwell, the fixed
pane above the front door. Those are the windows that change how the house feels
from inside, and they are the ones that get skipped year after year.

## What "clean" actually means on a Ferndale house

Worth being precise about, because the word covers three different jobs.

There is **dirt**, which is anything sitting on the surface — dust, pollen,
splash, cobweb, bird mess. It comes off with water and technique. Every clean
handles this.

There is **film**, which is dirt that has bonded with moisture and dried in
place. Industrial haze and dried field dust both end up here. Film needs a
detergent stage and physical contact; a pressure rinse alone will smear it.

And there is **deposit** — mineral spotting, and in the worst cases, etching.
Fresh deposit comes off with a dedicated hard-water treatment. Old deposit that
has been through enough wet-dry cycles begins to attack the glass surface
itself, and at that point it is no longer a cleaning problem. Etched glass looks
cloudy in the same places no matter how many times it is washed, and the honest
answer is that it is a replacement decision, not a cleaning decision.

We tell you which of the three you have on the quote, standing at the window,
before any money changes hands. A lot of the frustration people have with window
cleaning in this area comes from paying for a clean that was never going to fix
what was actually wrong with the glass.

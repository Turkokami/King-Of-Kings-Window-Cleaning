  - q: "Can a standing seam roof be pressure washed?"
    a: "It can be, and it should not be. The panel will survive the water; the coating may not. A factory finish is a thin cured film, and pressure at the seam edges and around flashings drives water into places detailed for gravity rather than force. The roof is cleaned with chemistry and low pressure, which is slower and does not trade service life for speed."
  - q: "Why does moss grow on a metal roof at all?"
    a: "Not on the panel — on what collects beside it. Needles and organic debris pack into the seam bases, the flashing junctions and the low-slope transitions, and once there is organic matter holding moisture, moss establishes in that rather than on the metal. Clear the debris and the moss has nowhere to live."
  - q: "How do I tell standing seam from a snap-together panel?"
    a: "Look at the seam and the eave. A mechanically seamed roof has a folded, often double-folded seam; a snap-lock panel has a cleaner rolled profile. Either way, if you cannot see screw heads across the panel face, it is a concealed-fastener system and it is in this family. Visible screws in rows means exposed-fastener corrugated or ribbed, which is a different profile and a different maintenance item."
  - q: "Will cleaning fix the chalky look?"
    a: "It improves it, and it does not reverse it. Chalking is the coating's binder degrading under UV and releasing pigment as loose powder. Washing removes the loose layer, so the colour reads truer immediately afterwards. The chalking already in the film stays, because it is the finish rather than something on it."
  - q: "What about oil-canning — the waviness across the panels?"
    a: "That is inherent to flat metal in wide panels and it is not damage and not dirt. It comes from the material, the substrate flatness and thermal movement, and it changes with the light and the viewing angle. Nothing in a cleaning process affects it in either direction."

    a: "Less than people assume. The seams are the structural and weatherproofing detail, and a distorted seam changes how the roof sheds water — that is a repair, not a blemish. Standing seam is the premium residential metal system and the one most likely to
be misunderstood by whoever is quoting to clean it. It is quiet, long-lived and
almost maintenance-free across the panel face — which leads people to assume it
needs nothing, right up until the details prove otherwise.

## How the system works

Panels run vertically from ridge to eave. Adjacent panels are joined by a raised
seam standing proud of the panel face, either mechanically folded on site or
snapped together, depending on the system.

The fasteners live underneath. Clips attach the panel to the deck and the seam
closes over them, so no screw penetrates the weather surface anywhere on the
field of the roof. That single decision is why the system lasts: on an
exposed-fastener roof, several hundred rubber washers are the service life, and
here there are none in the weather.

The panels are also free to move. Metal expands and contracts substantially
across a temperature range, and a standing seam system is detailed to let it,
which is why the panel face stays flat-ish and the seams do not tear.

For cleaning purposes, the consequence of all this is simple and it shapes every
decision: **the panel face is nearly self-clearing, and everything that matters
happens at the seams, the flashings and the transitions.**

## What collects where

**Seam bases.** The junction where a raised seam meets the panel face is a
continuous, shallow, upward-facing ledge running the length of the roof. Needles,
fine organic debris and grit pack into it and stay. This is where moss on a metal
roof actually lives — not on the metal, but in the organic matter lodged beside
it.

**Flashings and penetrations.** Ridge, hip, valley, chimney, skylight and vent
flashings are where debris concentrates and where water is being managed rather
than simply shed. They are also where a small amount of trapped organic material
holds moisture against a detail for months at a time.

**Low-slope transitions.** Where the pitch flattens — over a porch, a dormer
return, a bay — panels shed far less readily, and everything that would run off a
steeper section stops there.

**The north elevation.** Same rule as every other surface here: the side that
does not dry between rains carries the film.

**Anywhere near salt water.** Salt deposit is not a visual problem in the short
term and it is a corrosion problem in the long one, particularly at cut edges,
fixings and any point where two different metals meet.

## The rest of the metal family, for comparison

Knowing which roof you have changes the quote and the method, and most people are
not sure which they have.

**Exposed-fastener corrugated and ribbed** — the classic agricultural profile,
also common on outbuildings, shops and budget-conscious residential builds. Screws
pass through the panel face into the structure, each with a sealing washer.
Cheaper to install, shorter service life, and the washers are the maintenance
item: they harden, crack and shrink with UV and thermal cycling over fifteen to
twenty years. Cleaning these roofs means working around several hundred small
ledges, and it is the roof type where a look at the fasteners has the most value.

**Metal shingle and tile profiles**, stamped to imitate slate, shake or tile.
Used on higher-end residential work. More surface detail means more places for
debris to sit and a slower clean.

**Stone-coated steel** is its own case and worth flagging: a steel panel with a
granular stone chip surface bonded to it. It looks like tile and behaves, for
cleaning purposes, more like asphalt shingle than like metal — the granules can be
dislodged by pressure just as shingle granules can. Anyone treating a stone-coated
roof as ordinary metal is likely to damage it.

**Aluminium panels**, common near salt water for obvious reasons. Softer than
steel, dents more easily, does not rust but does corrode galvanically against
dissimilar metals.

**Copper and zinc**, on a small number of higher-end and heritage properties.
These patina by design, and the patina is the finish. Aggressive cleaning takes it
off unevenly and the result is worse than what you started with. Cleaned very
conservatively, and we will say plainly what we are not going to attempt.

## What cleaning will and will not change

**Comes off:** organic growth in seams and around fasteners, moss and lichen,
needle and leaf debris, streaking, general atmospheric film, salt deposit, pollen,
and most tannin staining.

**Improves:** a chalked surface loses its loose powder and looks considerably
better, and the colour reads truer.

**Does not change:** fading, chalking already in the coating, scratches, dents,
oil-canning, existing rust, or a coating that has failed. None of those are dirt.

The honest framing is that cleaning a metal roof is preservation as much as
appearance. Removing organic material and salt from the details is what stops
small problems becoming corrosion, and it is why a roof cleaned on a sensible
cycle reaches the service life the manufacturer had in mind.

## Metal on agricultural and outbuildings

A large share of the metal roofing in Whatcom and Skagit County is not on houses.
Barns, machine sheds, riding arenas, shops and pole buildings across the valleys
are overwhelmingly exposed-fastener corrugated or ribbed steel, often on large,
simple, shallow-pitched planes.

Those roofs are simpler to clean and have their own concerns. Fastener count is
high, so washer condition matters more. Spans are long, so foot traffic is more
constrained. Agricultural exposure — dust, ammonia in livestock buildings,
fertiliser and chemical drift — is harder on coatings than residential exposure.
And the roofs are frequently large enough that condition monitoring has real
value, because a small fastener problem found early on a barn roof is a very
different number from the same problem found late.

## The coating is the roof

It is worth saying plainly, because it governs every cleaning decision that
follows: on a metal roof, the steel or aluminium underneath is not what wears
out. The coating is. Everything else — the seam design, the concealed clips, the
thermal movement detailing — exists to keep water and fasteners away from a thin
cured film that is doing the actual work.

Two families dominate residential work here.

**PVDF finishes** (sold under names like Kynar 500 and Hylar 5000) are the
premium option. They hold colour and resist chalking markedly better over
decades, which is why they carry the longer finish warranties and why they are
specified on standing seam more often than on budget panel.

**SMP finishes** — silicone-modified polyester — are the common choice on
exposed-fastener agricultural and outbuilding panel. Tougher against abrasion,
cheaper, and noticeably more prone to fading and chalking in UV.

You usually cannot tell them apart by eye on a new roof, and you can tell them
apart very easily on a twenty-year-old one. The practical consequence for
cleaning is that chemistry is chosen conservatively unless the finish is known,
and anything aggressive enough to strip a tired SMP finish has no business on a
roof at all.


## At a glance

| Property | Standing seam metal roof |
|---|---|
| How panels join | Raised vertical seams, mechanically folded or snapped |
| Where the fasteners are | Concealed clips under the seam — none in the weather |
| Why it lasts | No rubber washers exposed; panels free to move with temperature |
| Coating families | PVDF (premium, holds colour) and SMP (tougher, chalks sooner) |
| Where debris collects | Seam bases, flashings, penetrations, low-slope transitions |
| Cleaning method | Chemistry at low pressure; foot traffic minimised |
| What will not come off | Fading, chalking in the film, scratches, dents, oil-canning, rust |

**What to check before booking:**

- What overhangs the roof — conifers make this a debris job on a cycle
- Visible green at seams or flashings, which is a debris report, not a metal one
- Whether the surface rubs off chalky on your hand at an accessible edge
- Whether you can see screw heads across the panel face — that is a different profile
- Any distorted seam, which affects how the roof sheds and is a repair

## What to look at before booking

Three things tell you most of what a quote needs to know, and you can check two
of them from the ground.

**What is overhanging it.** Conifers above a metal roof mean the job is debris
clearing on a cycle, not washing on a whim.

**Whether there is visible green at the seams or flashings.** Green on a metal
roof is a debris report, not a metal report.

**Whether the surface rubs off on your hand** at an accessible edge. That is
chalking, and it sets expectations about what the finish will look like

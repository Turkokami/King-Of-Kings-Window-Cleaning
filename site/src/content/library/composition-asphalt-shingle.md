Composition shingle — asphalt shingle, comp roof, three-tab or architectural — is
what most of this region is roofed in. It is inexpensive, it is fast to install,
it handles a complicated roof shape without difficulty, and it has one
vulnerability that determines every decision about cleaning it.

## What a shingle is actually made of

Modern composition shingle is a layered product, and the layers are worth knowing
because they explain everything that follows.

At the core is a **fibreglass mat**, which provides the tear strength and
dimensional stability. That mat is **saturated and coated in asphalt**, which is
the waterproofing. On top, pressed into the asphalt while it is hot, is a layer
of **ceramic-coated mineral granules**.

The granules are not decoration. They block UV from reaching the asphalt, they
provide fire resistance, and they carry the colour — the material underneath is
black whatever the roof looks like from the street. When asphalt is exposed to
sunlight it dries, loses its volatiles, becomes brittle and cracks. The granule
layer is the only thing standing between the binder and that process.

So the shingle's service life is, functionally, the life of its granule coverage.
Everything below follows from that one sentence.

**Three-tab** shingles are a single flat layer with cutouts that make three tabs
per strip — the older, thinner, cheaper product, common on housing stock here from
the 1980s and 1990s. **Architectural** or dimensional shingles are laminated from
two or more layers, which makes them heavier, thicker, longer-lived and visually
textured. Most re-roofs here are architectural now.

## How the roof keeps water out

Nothing on a composition roof is sealed. The waterproofing is geometry.

Each course overlaps the one below, so water arriving from above runs down the
surface and off the eave without reaching a joint. Adhesive strips bond each
course to the next once the sun has warmed them, which is what stops wind
lifting tabs — but the adhesive is a wind detail, not a water detail.

This matters for two reasons. Anything that lifts a course breaks the geometry
and lets wind-driven rain past it. And anything that pushes water *upward* —
a pressure washer, a hose held wrong, a brush worked uphill — is sending water in
the one direction the entire system was designed not to handle.

## What grows on it here

**Algae**, most often Gloeocapsa magma, is the dark vertical streaking almost
every older comp roof in this region carries on its shaded slopes. It is a
cyanobacterium, not dirt and not mould; part of what sustains it is limestone
filler within the shingle itself, and it spreads downward with the water, which is
why the streaks start high and run to the eave. It is a surface organism and it
responds to chemistry.

**Moss** is the more serious one, because it does not stay on the surface. It
establishes at the lower edge of a course where debris collects, grows into the
lap and lifts the shingle above it. That lifting is the damage.

**Lichen** bonds tightly to the granule surface and is the most stubborn of the
three, because removing it aggressively removes granules with it.

**Organic debris** — conifer needles above all — is upstream of all of this. It
holds moisture against the surface, collects in valleys and at flashings, and
gives moss the damp organic bed it needs to start.

## Why north slopes fail first

The same rule that governs walls governs roofs, and on a roof it is starker
because the two planes are identical in every other respect.

At this latitude a north-facing slope receives effectively no direct sun for
months. Through a wet season running October into May it does not dry between
rains. The south slope on the same house, laid the same day from the same
bundles, is frequently clear.

Add shade from conifers and the effect compounds — less light, more debris, and
needles that mat and hold water. A roof under mature Douglas fir on a north aspect
is working in the hardest conditions this region offers.

## What cleaning can and cannot do

**Comes off:** algae streaking, moss, lichen to a large degree, needle and leaf
debris, and general atmospheric film.

**Improves:** the colour reads truer once the darkening film is gone, and a roof
often looks years newer than it did.

**Does not change:** granule loss, bare patches, curling or cupping tabs, cracked
or brittle shingles, failed flashings, or a roof at the end of its life. None of
those are dirt, and a clean will make them more visible rather than less — which
is uncomfortable but useful, because it is a truer picture of the roof.

The method is chemistry and patience: the growth is killed, it dies, dries,
loosens and weathers off over the following weeks. The roof does not finish
looking clean on the day. That slow release is the point — it is what lets the
material leave without anything mechanical crossing the granule surface.

## When a roof should not be cleaned

There are roofs we decline, and it is worth knowing what they look like, because
the alternative is paying for a clean that shortens the roof.

**Visible bare patches.** Where the granule layer is already gone in areas, the
asphalt underneath is exposed and brittle. Treating it will not restore anything
and walking it will make the bare areas larger.

**Curling, cupping or clawing tabs.** Edges lifting away from the plane mean the
mat has shrunk and the shingle has lost flexibility. Those edges break underfoot
and they break under water pressure.

**Widespread cracking.** Fine map-cracking across the surface is the binder
telling you it has lost its volatiles. That roof is near the end, and the useful
spend is a roofer's opinion rather than a wash.

**A roof somebody has already pressure washed.** It is usually identifiable — an
unnaturally uniform light patch, or fan-shaped striping where a wand was swung.
There is nothing left to clean off; what remains is the mat.

In each case the honest recommendation is to clear the debris, keep the gutters
running and spend the money on getting a roofer up there instead.

## The part of the roof nobody sees

One thing that materially shortens a shingle roof here has nothing to do with what
is growing on top of it.

A composition shingle sheds heat upward and downward. If the attic below is
poorly ventilated, heat builds under the deck and cooks the shingle from
underneath, ageing the binder from the side nobody inspects. Blocked soffit vents
— often blocked by insulation pushed into them — and an inadequate ridge vent are
common, and the symptom on the roof surface is premature brittleness across a
whole plane rather than in the wet, shaded places you would expect.

It is outside what a cleaning visit addresses, and it is worth mentioning when a
roof looks older than its age, because cleaning a roof that is failing from below
solves the wrong problem.

|---|---|
| Construction | Fibreglass mat, asphalt saturant and coating, ceramic-coated granules |
| What the granules do | Block UV, provide fire resistance, carry the colour |
| Three-tab vs architectural | Single flat layer with cutouts, vs two or more laminated layers |

| Cleaning method | Chemistry and patience; pressure is never appropriate |
| What will not come off | Granule loss, bare patches, curling, cracking, failed flashings |

**Signs worth acting on:**

- Dark vertical streaking on the shaded slope — algae, treatable
- Raised green growth at course edges — moss, and it is lifting the shingle
- Coarse grit accumulating in the gutter year after year on a roof that is not new
- Tab edges lifting away from the plane
- One slope visibly worse than the other on the same house — aspect, not neglect

## The two cheap things that extend it

**Keep the debris off.** Needles in valleys and at flashings are where the damp
starts and where moss establishes. Clearing them is a blower-and-hands job, not a
cleaning job, and it is the highest-return maintenance on a shingle roof in this
climate.

**Keep the [gutters](/services/gutter-cleaning/) clear**, especially after any
roof treatment — everything killed up there comes down over the following two
months, and a treatment followed by no gutter clearing produces a blocked system
going into winter.

Ask anyone who cleans exteriors on the west side of Ferndale and they will tell
you the same thing: the glass out there behaves differently. It does not get
dirtier in the way a house under a tree gets dirtier. It goes flat.

This page is an attempt to be straight about that — what is actually at Cherry
Point, what we observe on the glass, and where the line sits between those two
things.

## What is actually there

Cherry Point is a heavy-industrial area on the Strait of Georgia shoreline, west
of Ferndale and north of Lummi Bay. It is one of the largest concentrations of
industry in Washington State, and it has three principal components.

**BP Cherry Point Refinery** — a major crude oil refinery, operating, with its own
marine terminal.

**Phillips 66 Ferndale Refinery** — a second refinery a short distance away, also
operating.

**The former Alcoa Intalco Works** — an aluminium smelter on roughly 1,600 acres,
sandwiched between the two refineries. It ceased operating in 2020, laying off
around 700 workers. Demolition began subsequently and was around 45% complete in
early 2026, with buildings and silos scheduled to come down through 2026 and soil
work into 2027. The site was bought by AltaGas, which has proposed a green
hydrogen facility there, currently on hold.

That last point is worth sitting with, because it means **the industrial mix at
Cherry Point has changed materially within the last five years and is still
changing.** Anyone describing the area as a fixed, static source is describing it
as it was, not as it is.

## What we observe on the glass

Here is the part we can speak to directly, because it is what the crew sees.

On properties on the western side of Ferndale and out toward Custer and Birch Bay,
glass acquires a film with three distinguishing characteristics.

**It is even.** Not splash, not spotting, not a run down from a sill — a uniform
veil across the entire pane. That uniformity is why it is so easy to miss and so
maddening once noticed.

**It shows in raking light.** In flat daylight the window looks fine. When low
winter sun comes in at an angle, the whole pane reads slightly grey, as though
looking through a stocking. This is the single most common way people first
describe it to us.

**It returns faster than it should.** Householders in the band routinely say their
windows "never look clean even after washing them." That is not a comment on their
cleaning — it is what a fine particulate film does when it is being resupplied.

Practically, this is why a schedule that works in Lynden does not work on the west
side of Ferndale. Houses in the affected band tend to want three visits a year
rather than two, and the clean lasts better if it is timed to the calmer,
wetter months.

The same film lands on solar panels, where it matters more than it looks, and on
siding, where it reads as a general dulling rather than as the green growth people
expect.

## What we will not claim

This is the part that separates an honest page from marketing copy, so it is
stated plainly.

**We have not measured anything.** We do not sample air, we do not analyse
deposits, and we do not own instrumentation. Nothing on this page is based on
measurement we performed.

**We cannot attribute the film to a specific source.** A house west of Ferndale
sits downwind of an industrial area, a working agricultural valley, a marine
shoreline and a highway. Fine particulate arrives from all of those, and telling
them apart requires laboratory analysis, not a squeegee. When we say "industrial
fallout" we are using the local shorthand for a phenomenon people describe, not
asserting a chain of causation we have established.

**We will not tell you it is harmful, or that it is harmless.** Air quality in
this county is monitored by the Northwest Clean Air Agency and the Washington
State Department of Ecology, both of which publish data and both of which are
qualified to speak on it. We are not. If the health question is what is actually
on your mind, they are the right call and we will say so rather than fill the
silence.

What we will do is tell you what your glass looks like, what comes off, what does
not, and how often it is likely to need doing.

## How it is removed

There is no special chemistry. It is a surface film, not a bonded deposit, and
that is good news.

On reachable glass, the ordinary method — solution, scrubber, squeegee — takes it
off in a single pass, and physical contact is what does the work. On upper
storeys, purified water through a pole rinses it and leaves nothing behind as it
sheet dries.

The one thing to avoid is a quick hose-down. Wetting a fine even film and letting
it dry redistributes it and, on hard water, adds mineral spotting on top. The
half-measure is worse than leaving it.

## Why the weather decides the day

Two patterns are worth knowing, because they explain why the same house looks
different week to week.

**Prevailing weather here moves off the water and inland.** That is why the
question of what sits upwind of a property is a real question in this part of the
county rather than an abstract one, and why the effect is asymmetric — the
industrial area is west of Ferndale, and the western side of town carries more of
whatever is moving inland than the eastern side does.

**Still, cold, clear weather concentrates whatever is in the air.** Winter
inversions in the lowlands trap air near the surface for days at a time: no wind
to disperse, no rain to wash out. Those are also the days of lowest sun angle, so
the accumulation and the lighting that reveals it arrive together. That
coincidence is why so many people first notice this in January and conclude
something changed.

Neither of these is a claim about emissions. They are ordinary facts about how air
behaves in a coastal lowland with a mountain wall behind it, and they are the
reason a fine film is a seasonal experience rather than a constant one.

## On solar panels it costs more than it shows

Worth separating out, because the consequence is different.

A fine even film on a window is an appearance problem. The same film on a
photovoltaic array is a production problem, and it compounds with the edge-band
soiling that flush-mounted arrays in this region collect along the bottom frame
anyway.

Arrays on the west side of Ferndale carry both at once: the general dulling across
the glass, and the concentrated band where water pools at the frame lip and
evaporates. The band is the expensive half, because cells wired in a string are
constrained by the least productive one, but the general film is what makes the
whole array quietly underperform its rating.

If output has drifted down over a couple of years with no fault reported and no
new shade, this is one of the first things worth ruling out — and it is cheap to
rule out, because a clean either changes the numbers or it does not.

**How to tell whether your property is in the affected band:**

- Stand inside on a bright low-sun morning and look at a pane at a shallow angle
- Check whether the film is even across the whole pane rather than splashed
- Compare a sheltered window with an exposed one on the same elevation
- Notice whether it returns within weeks rather than months after a clean
- On an array, look for the general dulling as well as the bottom-frame band

## Where the band actually is

We will not draw a map, because we have not measured one and a map implies
precision we do not have.

What we can describe is the pattern we encounter: it is most pronounced on the
western side of Ferndale and out toward Custer, present and lighter through
central Ferndale, and appreciably reduced by the time you are east of the freeway
or south toward Marietta. Birch Bay and the coastal strip carry it mixed with
salt, which is a different problem layered on the same one.

If you are unsure whether your house is in it, the raking-light test settles it in
about ten seconds: stand inside on a bright low-sun morning and look at a
south-facing pane at a shallow angle. Either the glass is clear or it is not.

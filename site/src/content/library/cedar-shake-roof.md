  - q: "Can a cedar roof be pressure washed?"
    a: "No. Cedar is soft, and pressure erodes the summerwood between the harder grain lines, leaving a furrowed surface that sheds worse than it did before and holds more water. It also opens the checking. A cedar roof that has been pressure washed is identifiable for the rest of its life."
  - q: "Will cleaning bring the colour back?"
    a: "No. Silvered cedar is UV-weathered wood, and the grey is the surface of the material rather than a coating on it. Removing moss, lichen and debris makes a roof look markedly better and markedly more cared for, and it stays grey. Anyone promising fresh cedar colour is describing a stain job, not a clean."
  - q: "Is moss worse on cedar than on a composition roof?"
    a: "Harder to deal with, yes. On composition the moss sits in the lap and lifts the course.   - q: "How long should a cedar roof last here?"
    a: "Less than the same roof in a dry climate, and the variable is not the cedar, it is the drying. A cedar roof that gets sun and airflow does well. Cedar is the roof that separates operators who know this region from operators
who do not. It is a minority of the housing stock and a disproportionate share of
the difficult jobs, it is concentrated in the older and higher-value
neighbourhoods, and it punishes the fast approach more than any other surface
here.

## What the material is doing

Western red cedar is naturally durable — it contains extractives that resist
decay, which is why it was used on roofs in a rain-forest climate in the first
place. Those extractives are also finite. They leach out over decades of
weathering, and an old cedar roof is progressively less protected than a new one
by exactly the mechanism that made it viable.

Three things happen to the wood as it ages, and all three matter for cleaning.

**It silvers.** UV breaks down lignin at the surface and rain washes the products
away, leaving the grey. This is weathering of the material, not a deposit on it,
and it is permanent.

**It checks.** Cedar shrinks and swells with moisture cycling, and the surface
develops splits along the grain. Those checks are where moss and lichen get a
foothold that no surface treatment fully reaches, and they deepen over time.

**It erodes.** Rain and UV wear the softer summerwood faster than the harder
latewood, so an old shake develops a ridged, furrowed surface. Anything abrasive
accelerates this enormously.

## Shakes, shingles, and how they are laid

**Shakes** are split rather than sawn — thicker, irregular, textured, with a deep
shadow line and considerable surface relief. **Shingles** are sawn, thinner and
flat-faced.

Both are traditionally laid over **spaced sheathing** — battens with gaps rather
than solid deck — so air can reach the underside and the roof dries from both
faces. That detail is the reason a cedar roof works in this climate, and the
reason a cedar roof over solid sheathing with no ventilation gap fails early.

Knowing which you have changes the job. Shakes have more relief and therefore more
places for needles and moss to lodge; shingles are flatter and shed somewhat
better but are thinner, so there is less material to lose before a split goes
through.

## Why moss on cedar is the hard case

On a composition roof the moss is on the surface and in the lap. On cedar it is
in three places at once: on the surface, in the lap between courses, and down
inside the checks of individual shakes.

That third location is what makes it different. It is inside an absorbent material
that is itself holding moisture, which means the growth has a permanently damp
substrate and a physical refuge. Surface treatment reaches what is on top and in
the joints. What is deep in a check keeps some foothold, which is why cedar
regrows faster than composition on the same property with the same exposure.

It also means the removal method cannot be mechanical. Scraping or brushing
breaks shake edges, opens checking, and removes the weathered surface layer that
is now doing the protecting. Levering material out from between courses widens
the gap that admitted it.

The approach that works is gentle, chemical and slow: treat, let it die, let it
dry, let weather take it out over the following months, and come back on a cycle
rather than waiting for it to look bad again.

## What honest expectations look like

Cedar is the surface where the gap between the marketing photo and the real
outcome is widest, so it is worth being blunt.

**Achievable:** moss, lichen and algae removed or killed; needles and debris
cleared from courses, valleys and around penetrations; a roof that reads as
maintained from the ground and that stops actively deteriorating from trapped
organic material.

**Not achievable:** original colour; a uniform appearance across a roof that has
weathered unevenly; removal of every trace of lichen, which bonds into the wood
itself; reversal of erosion, cupping, curling or splits.

**Not a cleaning question at all:** shakes that have cupped away from the plane,
courses with through-splits, missing shakes, and any roof where daylight is
visible from the attic. Those want a cedar roofer, and cleaning a roof in that
condition spends money in the wrong place.

## Treated cedar, and why it changes the job

Not all cedar roofing left the mill in the same condition, and it is worth
establishing which you have before anything is applied.

**Untreated.** The traditional product, relying on the wood's own extractives.
Most older cedar roofs in this region are this, and by the time they are thirty
years old the extractives are largely spent.

**Preservative-treated.** Pressure-treated with a fungicide or a water repellent
before installation. It resists decay and growth noticeably longer, and the
treatment depletes from the surface outward, so an old treated roof behaves more
like an untreated one at the face while still being protected deeper in.

**Fire-retardant treated.** Required in some jurisdictions and on some
assemblies, identifiable by a stamp on the butt if any original shakes are
accessible. The retardant chemistry is water-soluble in some older formulations,
which is a genuine reason to be conservative with volume and dwell time rather
than a theoretical one.

**Previously coated or stained.** A cedar roof that has been oiled, stained or
treated with a surface product at some point in its life has a partial film on it,
usually failing unevenly. Cleaning removes the loose remnants and reveals how
patchy it had become, which is an honest result and not always a welcome one.

Where the history is unknown — which is most of the time on a house that has
changed hands — the method defaults to the gentlest thing that works.

## Where cedar turns up here

Cedar is not evenly distributed across the territory, and knowing that shapes what
a crew expects to find.

It concentrates in the older and more wooded parts of Bellingham — Edgemoor,
Fairhaven and South Hill — and around Lake Whatcom, where mature conifer canopy,
lakeside humidity and mid-century housing stock combine into exactly the
conditions cedar finds hardest. Sudden Valley has a substantial share of it, on
lots often deeply shaded by second growth.

**Chuckanut is the concentration.** The stretch running south from Edgemoor along
Chuckanut Drive, down past Chuckanut Bay and up into the wooded lots on the
mountain side, is where this region's cedar problem is at its most acute — and it
is not one thing causing it, it is four stacked on each other. Mature conifer
canopy over most of the lots, so the roofs barely see sun. Marine air off the bay
holding humidity against everything. Steep, wooded ground that keeps the air
still where a flatter site would get a breeze through. And a housing stock with a
high share of architect-designed mid-century and later homes, which is precisely
the era and the taste that put cedar on roofs in the first place.

A cedar roof in Chuckanut is working harder than the same roof three miles north
on an open lot, and it wants a shorter cycle and a gentler hand. It is also where
getting the method wrong is most expensive, because it is where there is the most
cedar left to ruin.

That geography is the geography of the hardest moss pressure in the county, which
is not a coincidence — the same shade, damp and tree cover that made these
addresses desirable is what the roof is up against.

**Signs a cedar roof needs attention:**

- Green swelling between courses in the wet months, invisible by August
- Moss down inside the checks of individual shakes, not just on the surface
- Gutters filling faster than they used to with fine wood debris
- Cupped shakes standing proud of the plane, especially on the north slope
- Daylight visible from the attic — a roofer question, not a cleaning one

## What actually extends a cedar roof

Shade is the variable. A cedar roof in sun with airflow lasts; a cedar roof that
never dries does not — and nothing applied to the surface changes that arithmetic
as much as opening the roof to light and air does.

So the highest-return maintenance on a cedar roof in this region is unglamorous:
**trim back what overhangs it**, and **keep the debris off**. Needles held against
cedar are worse than needles held against asphalt, because the wood absorbs what
sits on it. Clearing valleys, the ridge line and the area around chimneys and
skylights two or three times a year does more than any product.

Keeping the [gutters](/services/gutter-cleaning/) running matters for the same
reason — a backed-up gutter keeps the bottom courses wet permanently, and the
eave is where cedar roofs fail first.

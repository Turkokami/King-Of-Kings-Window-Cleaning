If a house in Whatcom or Skagit County was built or re-clad after about 1975,
there is a good chance it is wearing vinyl. It is the default residential siding
in both counties, it is the most common surface we soft wash, and it is
straightforward — provided two questions get answered before anyone turns a hose
on it.

## How the material is built, and why it matters

Vinyl siding is extruded PVC hung on the wall rather than fastened tight to it.
Panels lock into the course below, are nailed through elongated slots, and are
deliberately left free to slide. That looseness is not sloppy installation; it is
the design. Vinyl moves substantially with temperature, and a panel nailed tight
buckles in July and pulls in January.

Two consequences follow, and both shape how the surface is cleaned.

**The wall behind it is not sealed.** Vinyl is a rain screen. Water gets behind
it routinely and by design, and the drainage plane and flashings underneath are
what actually keep the house dry. The panels shed the bulk of the weather
downward, course over course, exactly like shingles.

**The colour is the material, not a coating.** There is no paint film on
standard vinyl — pigment runs through the extrusion. That is why vinyl does not
peel, and it is also why a faded wall cannot be washed back to colour: the
surface layer itself has degraded.

## What actually grows on it

**Green algae** is the common case and the reason most people call. An even film,
worst on the north elevation and on anything under a canopy, heaviest at the
lower courses where splash keeps things damp.

**Mildew and mould** show up as black speckling and blotching rather than an even
film, concentrated in the dampest, most sheltered places — behind shrubs pressed
against the wall, under eaves, in corners.

**Lichen** turns up on older walls on the shaded side, and on the lower courses.

**Dirt splash** on the bottom two or three courses, thrown up off beds and paths
during heavy rain, often mixed with soil.

**Artillery fungus**, which is the one genuinely difficult problem. It lives in
hardwood bark mulch and fires spores several feet, and they bond to siding hard
enough that removal is partial at best. The remedy is mostly upstream: change the
mulch.

All of the above except the last sits on the surface. None of it is eating the
vinyl.

## What is not growth

Two things get called dirt and are not.

**Oxidation and chalking.** UV degrades the surface layer of the extrusion over
years, leaving a chalky film that rubs off on your hand and a colour that reads
washed out. Cleaning removes the loose chalk and usually improves appearance
considerably. It does not restore the finish, because there is no finish to
restore — a wall that has faded is not dirty.

**Staining from something running down the wall.** Rust from a fixing, tannin
from an overhanging conifer, run-off from a gutter joint, mineral spotting from
sprinkler overspray on hard water. Each of these is a different treatment, and
each is worth identifying at the quote rather than discovering mid-wash.

## Method, and why it is not pressure

Vinyl gets soft washed: low pressure, roughly garden-hose level, with a cleaning
solution matched to the growth, given time to dwell, then rinsed thoroughly. The
work is chemical. The water is a delivery and rinse mechanism.

The temptation with vinyl specifically is that it tolerates pressure better than
cedar, fiber cement or stucco do — the panel usually survives. What does not
survive is the wall behind it. Force drives water upward behind the laps, into
the assembly, past flashings detailed for gravity-driven water. In a climate this
wet a wall cavity does not dry out quickly, and the consequences arrive years
later as soft sheathing and rot at the sill rather than as a visible mark on the
siding.

Old vinyl adds a second reason. Panels embrittled by decades of UV crack under
force that newer panels absorb, and a cracked course is a replacement rather than
a repair, because matching twenty-year-old vinyl colour is largely a matter of
luck.

Planting below the work area is pre-wetted before anything is applied, sensitive
planting is covered, and everything is rinsed at the end.

## How vinyl compares to the other sidings here

**Fiber cement** — HardiePlank and similar, standard on newer construction. Durable
and washes well. The vulnerable parts are cut ends and anywhere the factory
coating is compromised. Low pressure only; it erodes under a jet.

**Cedar shingle and cedar lap** — common on older housing and around Chuckanut,
Edgemoor and the lake. Absorbent, so saturation has to be controlled. Silvered
cedar cleans up but does not return to fresh cedar colour.

**Painted wood** — throughout both counties on older stock. Sound paint washes
fine; failing paint comes off, and that is a conversation at the quote.

**Engineered wood** (LP SmartSide and similar), increasingly common on newer
builds. Cleans well, but the substrate is wood-based, so anywhere the coating is
breached is a place water should not be driven.

**Stucco and EIFS** — a smaller share of the stock here. Porous, and cracking is
common in older applications. Water behind stucco is a far more serious problem
than growth on it.

**Brick** — durable, but older lime mortar joints erode under pressure.

## Reading the age of a wall before washing it

Vinyl from the 1970s and 1980s and vinyl from the last decade are meaningfully
different materials, and the wall will tell you which you have.

**Panel thickness and rigidity.** Press a panel between the nail line and the
butt edge. Early vinyl is thin and flexes easily; modern panel is noticeably
stiffer, and insulated vinyl — foam-backed, increasingly common on newer builds
and re-clads — barely moves at all because there is rigid foam behind it.

**Sound.** Old thin vinyl rattles in wind and clicks with temperature change.

**The colour on a sheltered face.** Compare a section under a deep eave with the
same elevation in the open. A large difference means the exposed surface has
oxidised substantially, which sets expectations for how much washing will change.

**Whether it has already been damaged.** Look at the courses nearest a driveway,
a mower line or a heat source. Warping near a barbecue or a reflective window is
heat distortion, not dirt, and it is permanent.

None of this changes whether the wall can be washed. It changes how hard, and it
is the difference between a quote made from the ground and one made after
actually touching the building.

## When in the year to do it

Late spring through early autumn, for a practical reason rather than a
seasonal-marketing one: the wall needs to dry afterwards, and a solution needs
reasonable temperatures to work as intended.

Washing in the depth of winter is possible and it is rarely the best use of the
money — the growth will be back through the wet months regardless, and the result
never quite looks like it does in June. Late spring, after the pollen has mostly
finished, is the sweet spot on most houses here.


## At a glance

| Property | Vinyl |
|---|---|
| Typical era here | Anything built or re-clad from about 1975 |
| How it is fixed | Hung loose on elongated nail slots so it can move with temperature |
| Where the colour lives | Through the extrusion — no paint film, so it cannot peel |
| Main failure mode | UV embrittlement and surface oxidation (chalking) |
| Cleaning method | Soft wash only; low pressure, solution, dwell, rinse |
| What will not come off | Faded colour, chalked surface, artillery-fungus specks |
| Typical interval | Two to four years; less on a heavily shaded elevation |

**How to tell what you are looking at, from the ground:**

- Even green film across a whole elevation — algae, comes off cleanly
- Black speckling in corners and behind shrubs — mildew, also surface-level
- Chalk on your hand after rubbing a sunny wall — oxidation, will not wash back
- Small dark specks that resist everything — artillery fungus from bark mulch
- A dirty band on the lowest two or three courses only — splash, not growth

## Slowing the return

Removing the growth is the easy half.

Cut planting back from the wall — a foot of clearance makes a measurable
difference and two is better, because airflow is what dries siding. Trim what
overhangs. Fix sprinkler heads that hit the house. Keep the
[gutters](/services/gutter-cleaning/) clear so overflow staining stops recurring
every wet season. And if there is hardwood bark mulch within a few feet of a wall
that has black specks on it, that is the cause rather than a coincidence.

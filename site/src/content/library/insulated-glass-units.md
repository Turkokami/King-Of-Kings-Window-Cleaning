Almost every house in this region built or re-glazed in the last forty years has
insulated glass, and almost nobody thinks about it until one unit goes cloudy and
stays cloudy.

This page is mostly about that moment, because it is where the largest gap sits
between what a homeowner expects and what is physically possible.

## What an insulated glass unit actually is

A sealed unit is two panes of glass — sometimes three — held apart by a spacer
bar around the perimeter and bonded to it with a sealant, forming a cavity.

That cavity is not simply air. It is filled with dry air or an inert gas such as
argon, and the spacer bar itself is hollow and packed with a desiccant, which
absorbs whatever trace moisture is present at manufacture. The insulating
performance comes from the gas and from the coatings on the inner faces, not from
the glass, which is a poor insulator on its own.

Everything about the unit depends on the perimeter seal holding. It is doing two
jobs: keeping the fill gas in, and keeping water vapour out.

## How a seal fails, and what you see

Seals fail from fatigue. The unit heats and cools every day, the gas inside
expands and contracts, and the sealant flexes with it — thousands of cycles a
year, for decades. Eventually it develops a path.

Once there is a path, moist air enters, the desiccant absorbs it until it is
saturated, and after that every cold night condenses moisture onto the inner
faces of the glass.

The progression is fairly consistent. First, intermittent fogging visible only in
particular conditions — a cold morning, a sunny afternoon after rain — that clears
by itself. Then fogging that persists longer and appears more often. Then a
permanent haze. Finally, mineral deposit: the condensation runs, dries, runs
again and leaves etched tidemarks and a white bloom low on the pane, which is
the stage where the unit is beyond looking acceptable even on a dry day.

Two things make units fail sooner here. **Standing water** — a sill that holds
rain because it is blocked, badly sloped, or has lost its drainage path — keeps
water against the seal permanently. And **heat cycling** on south and west
elevations, which is why those go first on most houses.

## Why nobody can clean it

The haze is on surfaces two and three — the inner faces — inside a sealed
assembly. From the room you are looking through pane one. From outside you are
looking through pane four. There is no access to the cavity that does not involve
breaching the unit.

There are services that drill the unit, flush it and fit vents. It removes the
visible fog and it also converts a sealed insulating unit into a vented one, so
the insulating performance is gone and the glass is now open to outside air. It
is a cosmetic intervention on a dead unit, not a repair, and it is worth
understanding as that before paying for it.

The ordinary answer is replacement of the sealed unit alone, which is much
cheaper than replacing the window, and which a glazier handles from a measurement.

## How to tell a dead unit from a dirty window

Useful, because the two get confused constantly and the diagnosis decides who you
should be calling.

**Wipe both faces.** If the haze is unchanged from either side, it is not on a
surface you can reach.

**Watch it across a day.** A failed unit changes with temperature and humidity —
worse on a cold morning, better by afternoon, sometimes gone entirely in dry
weather. Dirt is indifferent to the weather.

**Look at the bottom edge.** Tidemarks, a white mineral bloom, or a visible line
where water has sat is condensation history. That is conclusive.

**Compare with its neighbours.** One hazy pane in a run of clear ones on the same
elevation is a unit that has failed, not a cleaning that was missed.

## The rest of the glass in the house

The other types show up on the same building and each behaves differently.

**Annealed glass** — older single panes, soft and forgiving to clean, usually in
wooden sashes that need care rather than force.

**Tempered glass** — heat-treated for strength and required by code in specific
locations: doors, glass beside doors, tub and shower enclosures, large low panes,
stair glazing. Look for the small etched stamp in a corner. It is stronger than
annealed and, counterintuitively, needs more care during cleaning, because the
tempering process can leave fine particles fused to the surface that a blade or a
hard pad will drag across the pane.

**Low-E coated glass** — a microscopically thin metallic coating, generally on an
inner surface of the sealed unit, that reflects heat. It gives the pane a faint
sheen or a slight colour cast that people routinely mistake for a film that will
not come off. It is not a film and it is not removable; on the rare units where
the coating is on an exposed face, it is also delicate enough that abrasives are
out of the question.

**Laminated glass** — two panes bonded to an interlayer, used in skylights,
overhead glazing and for security and sound. It stays in one piece when broken,
and a delaminating edge — a cloudy fringe creeping in from the perimeter — is its
own failure mode, distinct from a failed IGU seal.

## Condensation that is not a seal failure

Three different kinds of condensation turn up on a double-glazed window, and only
one of them means the unit is finished. Getting this wrong costs people money on
replacement units that were working properly.

**Inside the cavity** — between the panes, unreachable, unchanged by wiping. The
seal has failed. This page.

**On the room side of the inner pane** — wipes off, comes back. This is a house
problem, not a window problem: indoor humidity meeting the coldest surface in the
room. It is extremely common here through the wet months, worst in bedrooms and
bathrooms, and worst of all on single glazing and older aluminium frames with no
thermal break. The window is doing exactly what it should; it is simply the place
where the moisture in the air becomes visible first.

**On the outside face of the outer pane** — a dew film on a clear cold morning
that burns off by mid-morning. This one surprises people, and it is the most
counterintuitive fact about modern glazing: it means the unit is working *well*.
A high-performance unit keeps so little heat in the outer pane that the glass
drops to dew point like any other outdoor surface. Exterior dew on new windows is
a sign of good insulation, not a defect.

Worth running through before anyone is called, because two of the three have
nothing to do with the glazing.


## At a glance

| Property | Insulated glass unit |
|---|---|
| Construction | Two or three panes, perimeter spacer, sealed cavity |
| What is in the cavity | Dry air or argon, with desiccant in the hollow spacer |
| What the seal does | Keeps fill gas in and water vapour out |
| Why seals fail | Daily thermal cycling, accelerated by standing water at the sill |
| Which fail first | South and west elevations, and any sill that holds water |
| Can it be cleaned | No — the haze is on the inner faces, inside the assembly |
| The remedy | Replace the sealed unit alone, not the whole window |

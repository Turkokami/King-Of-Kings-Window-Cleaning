The usual version of this call: production is down measurably year over year, the
inverter reports no fault, nothing is shading the array that was not shading it
last year, and from the ground the panels look clean.

They usually are clean, across most of their area. The problem is not spread
evenly, which is exactly why it is hard to see and easy to underestimate.

## The edge band, and why it costs more than it looks like

The aluminium frame of a standard panel sits slightly proud of the glass. On the
lower edge that makes a shallow lip a few millimetres high, running the full width
of the panel.

Water running down the glass reaches that lip and stops. It pools there briefly,
then evaporates, and everything it was carrying — dust, pollen, roof grit, the
fine particulate that settles out of the air over a winter — stays behind. Each
rain adds to it. Over a season it becomes a dark, hard band along the bottom of
every panel in the array.

What makes that band expensive rather than merely untidy is how panels are wired.
Cells in a string carry the same current, so the least productive cell in the
string constrains the ones in series with it. A band of soiling along the bottom
edge is not a small fraction of the glass being dirty. It is a constraint sitting
across an entire string, on every panel, simultaneously.

Moss establishing at that same frame junction is the more serious version of the
same thing, and it holds moisture against a seal.

## Why this latitude makes it worse

Two things work against the self-cleaning assumption here specifically.

The first is mounting. A great many residential arrays in this region are
flush-mounted to the roof plane, taking whatever pitch the roof happens to be,
rather than being angled on tilt frames. On a shallow roof that is a shallow
panel, and a shallow panel sheds far less readily under gravity.

The second is timing. The wet season here is also the low-light season. Between
November and February the sun barely gets above the ridgeline in much of the
county and the array is producing very little regardless. The rain falling in
those months does rinse the glass — but the material it carries has all winter to
settle against the bottom frame, and there is no strong summer sun drying and
shrinking the deposit off. By the time production genuinely matters again in
March and April, the band is established, and it is immediately followed by
pollen season.

The practical consequence is that the single most useful clean of the year is the
one in late spring, and its value is concentrated in the months right after it.

## What else lands on an array here

**Pollen**, heavily, through spring — a fine even film that dulls the whole
surface rather than concentrating at the edge.

**Conifer debris.** Needles, pitch and fine organic matter from anything
overhanging. Pitch is the difficult one, because it does not rinse and it
attracts everything else.

**Agricultural dust** through the late-summer fieldwork season in the valley,
which behaves like the film on windows and settles overnight onto damp glass.

**Industrial particulate** on the west side of the county, fine and persistent.

**Bird droppings**, which are the one genuinely opaque soiling type — a single
dropping over a cell is a hard shadow rather than a dimming.

## Panels are not all the same glass

Not a detail most homeowners need, but it shapes how the work is done.

**Monocrystalline and polycrystalline framed panels** are the overwhelming
majority of residential installs here: tempered front glass, aluminium frame, the
raised perimeter lip described above, an anti-reflective coating. Standard
method.

**Frameless and dual-glass panels**, more common on newer commercial installs,
have no perimeter lip — so no edge band, and considerably less to clean. They are
also more sensitive to point loads, which reinforces the never-walk-on-them rule.

**Textured or patterned front glass**, used to reduce glare and improve capture at
shallow angles, holds soiling in the texture more than smooth glass does and takes
longer to clean properly.

**Thin-film panels** turn up occasionally on older or specialist installs. Same
principles, gentler still.

**Solar hot-water collectors** are not photovoltaic panels and are a different
thing entirely — glazed collectors clean much like glass, evacuated tube arrays
have their own fragility.

## What to do now

1. **Pull the inverter history rather than the headline number.** A gradual decline
   across a year reads as soiling; a step change on a date reads as a fault or new
   shade.
2. **Compare strings if the system reports them separately.** One string down and
   the rest fine is rarely dirt — dirt does not respect wiring.
3. **Check what has grown.** A tree clear of the array three seasons ago may not be
   now, and partial shade has the same string-level effect permanently.
4. **Look along the bottom frame from the ground**, at a shallow angle. The dark
   band is the expensive part.
5. **Do not walk on the array**, and do not rinse it with hard water on a hot day.
   Both cost more than the clean.
6. **Book for late spring**, once pollen has finished, so the clean sits ahead of
   the months that actually produce.

## What is worth checking before booking a clean

Soiling is the common cause but it is not the only one, and it is worth ruling
the others out rather than cleaning and being disappointed.

Check whether anything has grown. A tree that was clear of the array three
seasons ago may not be now, and partial shading has the same string-level
consequence as an edge band, permanently.

Check the inverter history rather than the headline number. A gradual decline
across a whole year reads as soiling; a step change on a date reads as a fault or
a new shade source.

Compare strings if the system reports them separately. One string down and the
rest fine is rarely dirt, because dirt does not respect wiring.

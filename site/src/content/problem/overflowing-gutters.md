  - q: "Why does it overflow at the front edge rather than the end?"

  - q: "How often does this need doing where I live?"
    a: "It is decided by what is overhanging the roof, not by the calendar. A house on open lawn with nothing above it can genuinely manage one visit a year. A house under mature Douglas fir needs four, and no gutter guard on the market changes that number."

The call almost always arrives during a storm, and it almost always comes with a
theory attached: the gutters are too small, or they were installed wrong, or the
roof is throwing too much water at them.

Usually none of those. The gutter is the right size and it was installed
correctly. It simply does not have the depth it used to, because something is
sitting in the bottom of it.

## What is actually in the trough

Clearing a loaded gutter in this region turns up three distinct layers, and they
behave differently.

On top is the obvious material — leaves, cones, twigs, the occasional tennis
ball. This is the layer people picture, and it is the least of the problem. Bulk
debris blocks an outlet when it happens to land on one, but it sits loosely and
water finds its way past.

Underneath is a dense felt of needles, compacted by rain into something closer to
a mat than a pile. This is the layer that causes overflows, and it is the one
that is invisible from the ground. It lies flat, it holds water like a sponge,
and it takes up the depth the trough needed in a downpour.

At the bottom is silt — roof grit, shingle granules, breakdown from the felt
above, and windblown dust. It is heavy, it holds moisture permanently against the
metal, and it is the reason a neglected steel gutter rusts from the inside.

A gutter carrying all three has lost most of its usable capacity while still
looking, from the driveway, entirely fine.

## Tree by tree — what is landing up there

Which trees are near the house changes the schedule more than anything else, and
it is worth knowing what you have.

**Douglas fir** is the defining problem of gutter maintenance in this region.
Sheds needles continuously with a heavy autumn drop, and the needles mat into the
felt described above. Also drops cones, which are large enough to block an outlet
by themselves.

**Western red cedar** sheds in flat sprays rather than individual needles, and
those sprays knit into an even more effective mat than fir does. Cedar debris
also breaks down slowly, so it accumulates rather than composting away.

**Western hemlock** produces very fine needles and small cones — the fine needles
pass through almost any guard mesh and go straight into building the silt layer.

**Big-leaf maple**, common through the river valleys and older neighbourhoods,
drops leaves large enough to bridge a downspout outlet whole, plus an enormous
quantity of seeds in spring that germinate happily in gutter silt.

**Alder**, which colonises disturbed and damp ground throughout both counties,
drops small cones and catkins that behave like gravel in a gutter.

**Birch and ornamental cherry** in planted neighbourhoods contribute fine twiggy
debris and blossom drop, which is a spring problem rather than an autumn one and
catches people who only clean in November.

## Telling a blockage from a capacity problem

Four checks, and they can mostly be made from the ground during rain.

**Where the water leaves.** Over the front edge along the whole run means the
trough is loaded. Over one corner only means fall or settlement. Behind the
gutter means a drip-edge or fascia problem, which is a different trade.

**Whether the downspout is running.** Put a hand on the downspout in heavy rain.
A silent, dry downspout under an overflowing gutter is a blocked outlet, not a
full trough — and that is a ten-minute fix rather than a whole-house clean.

**Whether it got worse gradually.** A gutter that coped two winters ago and does
not cope now has not shrunk. Progressive failure is accumulation.

**What is growing in it.** Anything green and rooted means silt deep enough to
hold a seed, which means the trough has been at reduced capacity for at least a
season.

## What clearing it actually involves

Bulk debris out by hand, then the trough flushed so the felt and silt layers
leave rather than simply being redistributed, then every outlet and downspout
checked individually by running water through it and confirming it discharges.

That last step is the one most often skipped, and it is the one that decides
whether the gutter still overflows next month. A clean trough feeding a blocked
downspout overflows exactly as enthusiastically as a dirty one.

Debris is bagged and removed rather than dropped into the beds below, and the
run is checked for loose spikes and separated joints on the way past, because a
ladder is already there and finding a failing hanger now is worth more than
finding it in February.

## What to do now

1. **Wait for heavy rain and watch where the water leaves.** Over the front edge
   along the whole run means a loaded trough. Over one corner means fall or
   settlement. Behind the gutter is a different trade.
2. **Put a hand on the downspout while it is raining.** A dry downspout under an
   overflowing gutter is a blocked outlet — a ten-minute fix, not a whole-house
   clean.
3. **Look for anything green and rooted in the trough.** That means silt deep
   enough to hold a seed, so the gutter has run at reduced capacity for a season
   or more.
4. **Count what overhangs the roof.** Douglas fir, cedar and hemlock set the
   schedule; open lawn sets a much cheaper one.
5. **Clear the outlets before the next front**, even if the full clean waits.
   Function first, appearance second.
6. **Book the clean before the wet season**, not after the first overflow.

## The part that is not about the gutter

The reason this is worth doing on a schedule rather than on a complaint is where
the water goes when the gutter stops working.

A functioning gutter collects roof runoff and delivers it to a downspout, which
moves it away from the building. A loaded one delivers the same volume straight
down the wall and into the ground at the base of the foundation, every time it
rains, through a wet season that runs October into May.

That is the mechanism behind a surprising share of the damp crawlspaces, stained
siding bands, settled paths and rotted fascia ends in this region — and none of
it presents as a gutter problem by the time anyone calls about it.

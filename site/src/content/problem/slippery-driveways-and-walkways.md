Most people book this job because the path looks tired. Most people should book
it because the path is slippery, and the two are the same surface at different
stages.

In a climate where the wet season runs October into May, a shaded walkway
develops a biological film and then keeps it — and a film that holds water is a
traction problem on a surface people use every day, including in the dark, in
January, carrying something.

## What is actually on the concrete

"Dirty" covers several different things, and they do not all come off the same
way. Knowing which you have is the difference between a satisfying result and a
disappointing one.

**Biological film** — the green, grey or black layer that makes a path look tired
and makes it slippery. Algae, with moss in the joints and lichen on older
surfaces. This is the bulk of what we remove and it responds well: a cleaning
solution kills the growth and the pressure lifts it off. On a shaded path this is
essentially the entire job.

**Embedded dirt and grit.** Ordinary soil worked into a porous surface over
years. Comes out with pressure and a surface cleaner, and this is where the
dramatic before-and-after line comes from.

**Oil and vehicle fluids.** A different problem entirely, because oil has soaked
*into* the slab rather than sitting on it. Fresh oil often lifts substantially
with a degreaser and heat. Old oil that has migrated deep into porous concrete
may lighten rather than vanish, and no amount of pressure changes that — the
staining is below the surface being cleaned.

**Rust.** From metal furniture, rebar, fertiliser, or well water used in
irrigation. Rust is a chemical stain and it needs a rust-specific treatment;
pressure alone does nothing to it and can drive it deeper.

**Tannin staining.** Brown or reddish discolouration under and around trees,
particularly from conifer needles, bark mulch, and fallen leaves left in place
over winter. Very common here. It responds to the right chemistry and stubbornly
resists the wrong one.

**Efflorescence.** The chalky white bloom that appears on concrete, brick and
pavers as mineral salts migrate to the surface and crystallise. It looks like
someone spilled something. It is the material itself, and it needs a specific
treatment rather than force.

**Paint, sealer failure and tyre marks.** Each its own approach, and each worth
identifying before rather than during.

## Why it is worse on some paths than others

Growth follows water and shade, and on a property the pattern is usually obvious
once you look for it.

The north side of the house, the strip under the hedge, the section of driveway
in the tree line, the steps beside the fence — these go first and go worst,
because they are the parts that do not dry between rains. The open middle of a
driveway in full sun can stay reasonably clear for years while the edges are
green.

Three things reliably accelerate it: a downspout discharging onto the slab rather
than away from it, a sprinkler head hitting the path daily, and organic debris
left in place over winter, which holds moisture and feeds what is underneath.

All three are fixable, and fixing them is worth more over five years than the
cleaning is.

## How flatwork is actually cleaned

A surface cleaner, not a wand. It is a rotating bar under a shroud that delivers
even pressure across a wide path and leaves no striping, because nothing is being
held close to one spot.

Where there is biological growth — which is most of the time here — a cleaning
solution goes down first and is given time to work. That is what kills the
growth at the root rather than blasting the visible layer off and leaving it to
return in months. Pressure then removes what the solution has released. Doing it
the other way round, pressure first and no chemistry, is why some cleaned paths
go green again in a single season.

Surfaces get chosen for: exposed aggregate, stamped and coloured concrete,
pavers with sand joints, brick, and older slabs already spalling all want less
pressure than a plain broom-finish path, and some want a soft wash instead.

Runoff is managed. Wash water on a driveway goes somewhere, and where it goes
matters here — Bellingham's municipal code prohibits wash water reaching a storm
drain, and the Lake Whatcom watershed carries its own restrictions. Planting
below the work is pre-wetted and rinsed afterwards.

## What to do now

1. **Test the surface wet, not dry.** Dry algae grips; the same surface wet is
   genuinely slick, which is why a path feels fine in August and catches someone
   in November.
2. **Grit the step you are worried about this week.** A stopgap, not a fix, and it
   does nothing about the growth underneath.
3. **Follow the shade.** The strip under the hedge and the section in the tree
   line go first; the open middle can stay clear for years.
4. **Check the downspout and the sprinkler heads.** A discharge onto the slab or a
   head hitting the path daily is upstream of the whole problem.
5. **Clear organic debris before winter**, because leaves left in place over the
   wet months feed next year's film.
6. **Book the clean, then fix the cause** — the cause is worth more over five
   years than the cleaning is.

## The honest limits

Cleaning removes what is on and in the surface. It does not resurface concrete.

A slab that is spalling, crazed, or has lost its cream layer will come out clean
and still look worn, because what you are seeing is the condition of the material
rather than dirt on it. Cleaning will make it markedly better and will not make
it new, and anyone promising otherwise is selling a resurfacing job under a
washing name.

Sealing is a separate decision and a separate visit. It is worth considering on
pavers and on coloured concrete; it is often not worth it on a plain utility
driveway, and it should never be applied to a surface that has not fully dried.

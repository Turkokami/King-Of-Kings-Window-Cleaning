The thing worth understanding about roof moss is that the visible cushion on top
of the shingle is not where the damage happens. It is the part you can see, and
it is the part everyone photographs, but the mechanism is underneath it.

## What moss actually does to a composition roof

A composition shingle roof works by overlap. Each course sits over the one below
it, and water arriving from above runs down the surface and off the edge without
ever getting to the joint. Nothing is sealed. The waterproofing is geometry.

Moss establishes at the lower edge of a course, where debris collects and
moisture lingers longest. As it thickens, it grows into the lap — the gap between
the shingle above and the shingle below — and it grows with enough force to lift.

Once a course is lifted, the geometry is broken. Wind-driven rain, which arrives
at an angle rather than straight down, gets under the lifted edge and onto the
layer that was never meant to be the waterproof one. In a region where storms
come off the water and the wet season runs October into May, that is not a
theoretical exposure.

It also holds water against the shingle permanently, which accelerates the ageing
of the mat itself, and it collects debris that keeps the whole area damp.

## Why the removal method matters more than the removal

Three approaches exist. Two of them cost you roof.

**Pressure washing** is fast, dramatic and the most common cheap quote in this
market. A composition shingle is a mat coated in adhered granules, and those
granules are the UV protection for everything underneath. Pressure strips them.
It also drives water up under the courses in exactly the direction the roof was
built not to handle. The result looks excellent for one season and has removed
years of service life.

**Aggressive scraping and stiff-brushing** does the same damage more slowly. On
courses moss has already loosened, dragging a brush across is finishing what the
moss started — and the moss most worth removing is at the eaves and in the
valleys, which is where a person on a roof has the worst footing.

**Lifting the bulk off by hand and treating the remainder** is the approach that
does not cost anything. Heavy growth comes away gently by hand — moss is not
rooted into the shingle the way a plant is rooted into soil, and once it is thick
it lifts in mats. What remains is treated, dies, dries, loses its grip and
weathers off over the following weeks. The roof gets steadily cleaner without
anything mechanical being dragged across it.

That last part takes patience and it is the reason a good moss job does not look
finished on the day it is done. It looks finished two months later.

## Not every roof behaves the same way

The lifting mechanism above is specific to overlapping courses, so it plays out
differently depending on what the roof is made of.

**Cedar shake and shingle.** The worst case, and common on older housing around
the lake, Chuckanut and Edgemoor. Wood is absorbent, so moss is growing in a
material that holds moisture itself, and it establishes deep in the checking and
splits of weathered shakes as well as between the courses. Cedar cannot take
aggressive treatment or aggressive removal — shakes split, and the material is
already the thing decaying.

**Metal.** Standing seam and corrugated panels offer moss no foothold, but the
seams, the fixings and the low-slope transitions collect debris and moss
establishes in that. The concern there is the factory coating, which is what
makes the roof last.

**Concrete and clay tile.** Tile is porous and moss establishes readily in the
surface and the laps. The tile is not really threatened, but the weight of a
heavily mossed tile roof and the moisture held against battens and underlayment
are. The bigger risk on tile is foot traffic during the work — it breaks underfoot
far more readily than asphalt.

**Torch-down and low-slope membrane.** Growth is usually algae and standing
debris rather than moss proper, the vulnerable points are the seams, and the
useful part of the visit is often clearing the drains.

## What to do now

1. **Look at the north slope in November, not August.** Moss is grey, shrunken and
   near-invisible in dry weather and obvious in the wet.
2. **Check the gutters as a proxy.** A gutter filling faster than it used to is
   often reporting moss debris before the moss itself is conspicuous.
3. **Look into the valleys.** They collect first and worst, and a valley
   concentrates the water from two roof planes.
4. **Put the stiff brush down.** Scrubbing a course moss has already loosened
   finishes what the moss started.
5. **Get a quote that names the method**, not just the price — hand-lift plus
   treatment, or a pressure wash that costs you years of roof.
6. **Book the gutters for two months after**, because everything killed up there
   comes down over that window.

## What to do between treatments

A few habits genuinely extend the interval.

**Look at the north slope in November, not August.** Moss is dormant, shrunken
and grey in dry weather and near-invisible from the ground. It is green, swollen
and obvious in the wet months. Judging your roof in late summer is judging it at
the moment it lies to you most convincingly.

**Watch the gutters as a proxy.** A gutter filling faster than it used to is
usually telling you the roof above it is shedding more organic material than it
used to, and that is often moss debris before the moss itself is conspicuous.

**Keep an eye on the valleys.** They collect first and worst, and they are where
lifted courses do the most damage, because a valley concentrates the water from
two roof planes.

**Book the follow-up on a cycle rather than on appearance.** Three to five years,
set by shade and elevation rather than by how it looks from the driveway.

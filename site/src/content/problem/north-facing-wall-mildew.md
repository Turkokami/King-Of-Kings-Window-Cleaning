Almost every house-washing call in Whatcom and Skagit County is really a call
about one elevation. The north wall has gone green, the rest of the house looks
fine, and the homeowner assumes they have let something slip.

They have not. It is aspect, not neglect, and it is worth understanding why
before deciding what to do about it.

## Why the north wall specifically

At this latitude the sun tracks low across the southern sky. A north-facing wall
receives effectively no direct sunlight for months at a time — not reduced sun,
none. It is the last surface on the property to dry after rain and the first to
be damp again when the next front comes through.

Bellingham gets around 36 inches of rain a year, and roughly 80% of it falls
between October and March. For that half of the year a north wall is
functionally never dry.

Algae needs three things: moisture, a surface, and light. Not much light — far
less than a plant needs. A permanently damp north wall supplies all three, which
is why the growth appears there first and worst, and why it appears on
well-maintained houses.

## What makes it worse

Several things stack on top of the basic aspect problem, and most of them are
fixable.

**Planting against the wall.** The single most common aggravating factor we see.
Shrubs pressed up against siding block airflow, hold humidity against the
surface, and splash soil onto the lower courses in heavy rain. A foot of
clearance makes a measurable difference; two is better. Airflow is what dries a
wall, and a shrub against it prevents that permanently.

**Overhanging trees and tall hedging** extend the shaded, non-drying zone around
the building, which is why growth often wraps onto the east and west elevations
under the canopy line rather than stopping neatly at the corner.

**Gutter and downspout discharge** landing on or splashing back onto a wall keeps
one patch permanently wet, and that patch will be visibly worse than the wall
around it. If you see a concentrated green band under a gutter joint, the gutter
is the cause and the wall is the symptom.

**Sprinkler overspray** hitting siding does the same thing daily, and on hard
water it adds mineral spotting to the biological growth.

**Prevailing weather.** Wind-driven rain here comes predominantly from the south
and west, so those elevations get wetter — but they also get sun, so they dry.
The north wall gets less rain and holds what it gets.

## Telling growth from damage

Three different things get called "the north side looks bad," and only two of
them wash off.

**Algae** is the even green or grey-green film. Surface-level, comes off cleanly,
the most common by a wide margin.

**Mildew and mould** are the black speckling and blotching. Concentrated in the
dampest, most sheltered areas — behind shrubs, under eaves, in corners. Also
surface-level.

**Oxidation and chalking** is not growth at all. UV degrades the surface of vinyl
and painted siding over years, leaving a chalky layer that rubs off on your hand.
Washing removes the loose chalk and usually improves appearance considerably, but
it does not restore the finish. A wall that has faded is not dirty.

The useful test at the quote is simple: run a wet finger down the wall. If it
comes away green or grey, it is coming off. If the wall looks the same colour wet
as dry, that is the material, and washing will not alter it.

## How it comes off

Soft wash. Low pressure — roughly garden-hose level — with a cleaning solution
chosen for the growth and the substrate, given time to dwell, then rinsed
thoroughly.

Not a pressure washer. Siding is built on the same principle as a shingle roof:
overlapping courses that shed water downward. Force drives water *upward and
sideways behind the laps*, into the wall assembly, past flashings detailed for
gravity-driven water. In a climate this wet, water in a wall cavity does not dry
out quickly, and the failures show up years later as soft sheathing and rot at
the sill.

Planting below the work area is pre-wetted before anything is applied, sensitive
planting is covered, and everything is rinsed at the end. Pre-wetting matters
more than people expect — a plant already saturated with clean water absorbs far
less of whatever lands on it afterwards.

## What to do about the cause

Removing the growth is the easy half. Slowing its return is the useful half, and
none of it is a product:

- **Cut the planting back from the wall.** Highest return of anything on this
  list.
- **Trim what overhangs.** Less shade, less drip, less organic debris landing on
  the elevation below.
- **Fix the sprinkler heads that hit the house.**
- **Keep the [gutters](/services/gutter-cleaning/) clear**, so overflow staining
  stops recurring every wet season.

Expect two to four years between washes on a typical elevation here, and less on
a heavily shaded one. That interval is a fact about the climate rather than a
failure of the last clean.

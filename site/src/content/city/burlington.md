Burlington grew up at a crossing. Two railroad lines met here in 1890 and gave the
place its old nickname, the Hub City, and the town has kept that character since:
I-5 runs through it, the Skagit River bends past its southern edge, and a large
retail strip grew along Burlington Boulevard after the Cascade Mall opened in
1989. It is a working, busy, practical town, and its houses are a working mix.

This page covers what we see on Burlington exteriors, which of our services each
problem calls for, and what the town's own stormwater rules mean for how the
work is done.

## What kind of housing does Burlington have?

A younger and more varied stock than most people expect of a valley town. The
median year built across Burlington's housing is 1993, and 1,517 of its 4,246
units went up in 2000 or later. A house of that age has usually had long
enough to collect moss, algae and grime, but not so long that its roof and
siding are past the point where a careful clean still pays off.

The more unusual number is the split between houses and everything else. Only
2,137 of those 4,246 units are single-family detached homes, which leaves
roughly half the town living in attached or multi-unit buildings of one kind or
another. And of the 3,925 occupied homes, 2,178 are rented rather than owned.

That changes who is likely to call. In Burlington the person booking a gutter
clean may well be a landlord between tenants, a property manager with several addresses, or
an owner who lives in another county and wants the house kept up without
driving over to check on it.

## What do the river and the valley do to a Burlington exterior?

The Skagit River forms the city's southern and southeastern boundaries, and it
turns to the southwest here as it crosses a floodplain about eleven miles wide
and nineteen long. Burlington sits low and open on that ground. The town has
flooded badly several times, in 1909, 1917, 1921, 1990 and 1995, and the same
flat terrain that let the water spread also shapes how a house weathers.

Three things follow from it:

- **Long damp spells on shaded walls.** Low, level ground with open fields around
  it holds moisture in the cool months, and a north or east elevation that never
  sees direct sun stays wet long enough for green growth to take hold.
- **Farm dust in the drier months.** Skagit County farmers work roughly 90,000
  acres and grow more than 90 crops, and fieldwork on that scale puts fine soil
  into the air that settles on glass and siding.
- **Traffic grime near the corridors.** Homes close to I-5 and the busier
  commercial roads collect a grey road film on the side facing the traffic.

None of this is dramatic. It is steady, and steady is what fills a gutter and
greens a wall.

## How much rain does Burlington actually get?

Burlington has no long-term weather station of its own, so the honest figure
comes from the nearest one, the Mt Vernon 3 WNW station in the neighbouring town. Over
its record from 1956 to 2005 that station averaged 32.58 inches of precipitation
a year. November was the wettest month at 4.48 inches and July the driest at
1.16 inches.

For exterior work, the useful reading of those numbers is the shape of the year
rather than the total. There is a genuine dry window in high summer when roofs
and siding can be treated and left to dry properly, and a long wet stretch from
autumn into spring when gutters carry the most water and shaded surfaces grow
the fastest.

Put simply, the Burlington year sorts itself into three working seasons:

1. **July and August** for roof treatments and house washing, when surfaces dry
   between applications and a treatment is not rinsed off by the next shower.
2. **October into early November** for gutters, once the leaves are down and
   before the wettest month arrives.
3. **Spring** for windows, after the winter film and before the long evenings
   when people actually look through their glass.

## What are Burlington's rules on wash water?

Stricter in detail than many homeowners realise. Burlington Municipal Code
14.05.070(B) prohibits putting anything other than storm water into the city's
drainage system. Subsection (C) goes on to name soaps, detergents and ammonia,
and steam-cleaning wastes, among the illicit discharges. The one relevant
allowance is in (E)(4): routine external building wash-down is permitted only if
it does not use detergents.

Where that water goes gives the rule its weight. Most of Burlington's runoff
discharges into Gages Slough, the channel the Tammi Wilson Memorial Trail
follows, and from there it is pumped into the Skagit River. The city rates
cleaning up the slough a very high priority.

In practice that means we plan the rinse path before we start: drain inlets near
the work get covered, rinse is steered onto lawn or planting beds, and cleaning
solution is used only where the surface genuinely needs it. The detail is on our
page about [wash water and storm drains](/compliance/wash-water-and-storm-drains/).

## Does the drinking water matter to exterior cleaning here?

Less than it would on a private well. Burlington is supplied by Skagit PUD's
Judy Reservoir system, which draws on four Cultus Mountain creeks, with a pump
station on the Skagit River as a supplemental source. No hardness figure for
that supply is published that we could verify, so we make no claim about it.

It does not change our method either way. We carry filtered, purified water for
glass so that whatever comes out of a customer's tap never dries on a pane we
have just cleaned.

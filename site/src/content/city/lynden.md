Lynden sits about fifteen miles north of Bellingham and five miles short of the
Canadian border, and it asks for a slightly different kind of exterior work than
the city this company started in. Its houses are younger on average. Its
rainwater, once it leaves your downspouts and reaches the street, heads for
Fishtrap Creek. And its municipal code has something specific to say about what
may go down a storm drain along with that rain.

What follows is what those three facts mean for the siding, gutters, roof and
glass on a Lynden house, with a link to each service page where you want more.

## What are Lynden's houses like?

Mostly newer than a first look at Front Street suggests. The Dutch storefronts and
the windmill there are the product of a 1980s remodel, so even the most
photographed buildings in town are younger than they appear.

The Census Bureau's latest five-year estimate puts the median year built for
Lynden housing at 1997. Of 6,210 housing units, about 2,773 went up in 2000 or
later, which is roughly 45 percent of the town, and 325 of those date from 2020
onward. HistoryLink records that the population grew by nearly 60 percent during
the 1990s alone, and the housing count carries the mark of it.

There is an older layer as well. The same estimate counts 483 units built in 1939
or earlier and another 410 from the 1950s. On those houses we slow down and look
closely at what the siding, trim and glass are made of before choosing a method,
because an old surface forgives far less than a factory finish does.

Detached single-family homes account for 4,082 units, about two-thirds of the
stock, with 347 attached single units and 146 mobile homes alongside them. Around
two-thirds of occupied homes, 3,984 of 6,018, are lived in by their owners. An
owner-occupied house tends to be looked after on a cycle rather than cleaned once
for a sale, and a cycle is how we would suggest planning it.

### Why this page names no neighbourhoods

We could find no official list of named Lynden neighbourhoods, and we would
rather leave a gap than invent one. The only named area we could find in the code is
Pepin Creek, a planning sub-area with its own zoning chapter (LMC 19.18) created
by Ordinance No. 1574 in 2019. That is a zoning designation, not a neighbourhood
name, and we will not dress it up as one.

## What does the weather here do to a house?

It keeps the shaded side wet for half the year. The long-record weather station
nearest Lynden is the Western Regional Climate Center's cooperative station at
Clearbrook, which averages 46.71 inches of precipitation a year over a record
running from 1903 to 2016. It sits in the area rather than in town, so treat its
figures as the local pattern rather than a gauge on your roof.

The shape of that pattern matters more than the total. November averages 6.17
inches and December 6.22, while July manages only 1.47. The same station averages
16 inches of snow a year, most of it in December and January.

On a house, that calendar shows up in predictable places:

- **The lower courses of siding**, where rain splashes back off beds and paths
  and the boards stay damp long after the wall above has dried
- **Any wall that gets little sun**, which can go green within a couple of wet
  seasons even on siding that is nearly new
- **Gutters and downspouts**, which have to carry the heaviest months of rain and
  then sit under snow and ice through the coldest ones
- **The roof's shaded slope**, where moss and algae get the longest run of damp
  days to establish
- **Glass and frames**, where splash, pollen and organic film build in the
  corners faster than on the open pane

## What else lands on a Lynden exterior?

Farming is part of the answer. Lynden's economy turned from logging to
agriculture around the turn of the twentieth century, dairy and berry farms above
all, and the county's berry output is still remarkable: the Whatcom Conservation
District credits Whatcom County with nearly 85 percent of the nation's red
raspberries, close to 48 million pounds a year. A house that faces working land
can collect a finer, drier film on its windows and siding than one set among
other houses, and when we can see that on the day, we plan for it.

Trees are the other part, and the town has more of them than a drive past the fields suggests.
Lynden City Park sits among tall conifers on Fishtrap Creek, and Berthusen Park,
whose 236 acres the Berthusen family left to the city, includes old-growth
forest. Whether your own lot carries real needle and leaf pressure depends on
what stands near it, which is one more reason we look before we price.

## How do you wash siding on a Lynden house?

With low pressure and a cleaning solution that does the work, not a narrow jet
that forces water behind the boards. That is how the one Lynden job we have
documented was done: in August 2026, white lap siding with green growth on the
lower courses and along a shaded side wall was soft washed back to clean, with
before and after photos taken on the day and now in the [gallery](/gallery/).

The pattern on that house is the one this climate produces, growth low down and
growth on the side that sees least sun. Our [Lynden house washing
page](/locations/lynden/house-washing/) covers method, rinse handling and timing in
full.

## What does gutter clearing involve here?

Hand clearing, bagging and removal, followed by a flush of each downspout to
confirm the water actually leaves the run. The job matters most before November,
when the heaviest rain of the year arrives, and again after any stretch of snow
and ice has sat in the troughs. There is a local rule involved as well: Lynden's
code counts collected leaves and branches as an illicit discharge to the storm
system, so debris goes in a bag, never down the street drain. The detail is on the
[Lynden gutter page](/locations/lynden/gutter-cleaning/).

## Can you clean a Lynden roof?

Yes, using a soft wash rather than a pressure washer, which on shingles strips
granules and shortens the roof's life. Moss and algae get treated, the heavier
growth is removed by hand, and the rest breaks down and rinses off over the
following weeks of weather. Treatment goes on in the drier part of the year so it
has time to work before the wet months return. We are cleaners, not roof
inspectors, and if we see something that looks like damage we will point it out
and suggest you have a roofer look at it. Read more on the [Lynden roof cleaning
page](/locations/lynden/roof-cleaning/).

## What about the windows?

Interior and exterior, screens and tracks, with purified water on a pole for
upper storeys and squeegee work on glass we can reach by hand. Lynden's drinking
water is treated Nooksack River water, and the city's water quality report
publishes no hardness figure, so we make no hard-water claims about the town. We
carry our own purified water regardless. The [Lynden window page](/locations/lynden/window-cleaning/)
goes into access, larger modern panes and older sashes.

## What does Lynden's stormwater code ask of us?

More than people assume, and it is specific. Chapter 13.24 of the Lynden
Municipal Code lists illicit discharges to the city storm system, and three items
on that list touch exterior cleaning directly:

1. Steam-cleaning wastes (item 14)
2. Soaps, detergents or ammonia (item 15)
3. Collected leaves or branches (item 24)

A separate provision, 13.24.160(C)(4), treats routine external building
wash-down as a conditional discharge, allowed only when no detergents are used.
The city's own stormwater page adds plain advice to clean up debris before and
after pressure washing.

The reason is downstream. Most of what enters a Lynden storm drain ends up in
Fishtrap Creek and then the Nooksack River, the same river the city treats for
its drinking water. In practice we decide on the quote where rinse water will go,
keep detergent-bearing water on lawn and beds rather than hard surfaces that
slope to the street, and leave the pavement swept. Our [wash water and storm drain
page](/compliance/wash-water-and-storm-drains/) sets out the wider approach.

## Can everything be done in one visit?

Usually, and it is generally the cheaper way to do it. A full exterior visit
runs from the top down: roof, then gutters, then the walls, then the glass. Done
in that order, nothing loosened from the roof or the troughs lands on siding or
windows that have just been cleaned, and one setup covers the lot.

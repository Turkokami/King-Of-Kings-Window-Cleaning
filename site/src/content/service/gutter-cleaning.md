Gutters are the least interesting part of a house and the most expensive one to
ignore. A blocked gutter does not announce itself. It works fine in light rain,
overflows in heavy rain when nobody is standing outside watching, and the damage
it causes — soaked fascia, wet crawlspace, undermined footings, siding stained
from the top down — arrives years later, priced as a structural repair.

In this part of Washington the job is also genuinely different from gutter
cleaning in most of the country, for one reason: it is not about leaves.

## The local problem is needles, not leaves

Almost every piece of gutter-cleaning advice written for a national audience
assumes a deciduous autumn — leaves fall over a few weeks, you clear them, you
are done until next year. That model does not describe a house under Douglas fir,
western red cedar or hemlock.

Conifers shed **year-round**, with a heavier drop in late summer and autumn but
never a clean stop. The needles are small, they are stiff, and critically they
**mat**. A layer of fir needles knits into a dense felt that holds water rather
than letting it through, packs into the downspout outlet, and forms a plug that a
leaf blower will not move. Under that mat, silt accumulates and turns into a soil
layer — which is why gutters in this region grow moss, and occasionally grow
seedlings.

Big-leaf maple adds a second problem in the neighbourhoods that have it, because
the leaves are large enough to bridge a downspout outlet on their own and the
seeds get into everything.

The practical consequence: **a gutter here can look clear from the ground and be
completely blocked.** The mat sits at the bottom, below the line of sight from
below, and the first evidence is water coming over the front edge in a downpour.

## How much water is actually involved

Worth having a number, because the volumes surprise people.

An inch of rain falling on a 1,500 square foot roof is roughly 900 gallons of
water. That has to leave the roof, travel the length of the gutter, turn down the
downspout and get away from the house. Bellingham sees something on the order of
three feet of rain a year; the foothills around Deming, Acme, Maple Falls and
Glacier see substantially more.

Every one of those gallons that does not make it into the downspout goes over the
front edge of the gutter and lands directly at the base of the wall — the exact
place a house is least able to cope with it.

## What blocked gutters actually damage

In rough order of how expensive the surprise is:

**Fascia and soffit.** The board behind the gutter stays wet, and wet wood in this
climate does what wet wood does. Rot in the fascia often means the gutter's
fixings have nothing sound to hold onto, so the repair is the board *and* the
gutter run.

**The wall and the siding.** Water sheeting over the front edge runs down the
siding, and on the shaded elevations it keeps the surface damp long enough for
algae and mildew to establish. Staining that starts under a gutter is a
diagnostic sign, not a cleaning problem.

**The foundation and the crawlspace.** This is the expensive one. Water dumped at
the base of a wall saturates the ground against the footing, and in a region with
this much winter rain a crawlspace does not dry out between events. Damp
crawlspaces bring their own long list of consequences.

**The gutter itself.** Standing water plus organic debris is corrosive, and a
gutter carrying a permanent load of wet silt is heavy enough to pull its own
fixings out of the fascia over a few seasons.

**Ice, in the foothills.** At elevation — Glacier, Maple Falls, Kendall — a gutter
holding water going into a freeze is a gutter holding ice, and ice is much
heavier and does not care about the fixings.

## What the job involves

- **Hand-clearing** the full run, both sides of the house, with debris bagged
- **Debris removed from site** — not blown onto the roof, the deck or the beds
- **Downspouts cleared and flow-tested**, every one
- **Gutter interiors wiped down** of the silt layer where it has built up
- **Outlets and elbows checked** for the plug that forms just past the bend
- **A condition note** on anything we can see — loose spikes, separated seams,
  sagging runs, rust, rot behind the gutter
- **Ground left clean**, splash rinsed off siding and glass

Quoted separately: gutter brightening (the exterior face), roofs with genuinely
difficult access, and buildings over two storeys.

## Why hand-clearing rather than blowing

Blowing gutters out is faster, and on a house with light leaf debris it is a
legitimate method. It is the wrong method here for three reasons.

First, it does not clear a matted needle plug. A blower moves loose material; a
felt of wet fir needles is not loose material.

Second, it puts everything it does move onto the roof, the siding, the windows,
the deck and the planting — and on a roof, a redistributed layer of organic debris
is exactly what moss wants.

Third, and most usefully, **hand-clearing means somebody sees the gutter.** Every
pulled spike, separated seam, corroded section and soft patch of fascia we have
ever flagged was found because someone was looking into the gutter rather than
pointing a machine at it from six feet away.

## Downspouts are where the failures hide

A gutter is the visible half of the system and the half that gets the attention.
Most of the failures are in the other half.

The classic one is a plug at the **outlet** — the hole in the gutter floor where
the downspout starts — or just past the first **elbow**, where debris travelling
in water hits a change of direction and stops. Neither is visible from the
ground. Both will let light rain through and back up completely in heavy rain,
which is why a homeowner's experience of a blocked downspout is usually "it was
fine and then suddenly it wasn't".

Then there are **underground lines**. A lot of houses here run downspouts into
buried pipe going to a storm connection, a dry well or a daylight outlet
somewhere down the slope. Those lines block with silt and root intrusion, and
when they do, the water has nowhere to go but back up the downspout and over the
gutter. We flow-test to the ground and tell you what happens; if it backs up
there, the problem is in the buried line and it is a drainage job.

## Gutter guards, honestly

Guards are sold as the end of gutter cleaning. In this region they are not, and
it is worth being straight about what they do and do not do.

**What they do well:** keep large deciduous leaves and twigs out, reduce the
frequency of clearing, and keep birds and rodents from nesting in the gutter.

**What they do not do:** stop conifer needles. A fir needle is thin enough to
drop through most mesh apertures, and where it does not, needles mat *on top of*
the guard instead — which is worse, because water then runs over the whole
assembly rather than into it, and clearing the mat means removing the guard.

Fine grit and shingle granules also pass through nearly everything and build the
silt layer underneath, which is where moss starts.

The honest summary: guards are a reasonable investment on a house under
deciduous trees, a mixed one under conifers, and never a substitute for someone
looking at the system twice a year.

## When to book

**Late autumn**, once the deciduous drop has finished, is the most important
visit of the year. Going into a Whatcom or Skagit winter with clear gutters is
the whole point of the exercise.

**Spring**, to clear what came down over winter and the seed and blossom drop
that follows.

**Under conifers, twice more than that** — realistically three to four visits a
year on a heavily treed lot. This is the single most common thing homeowners
underestimate here.

**After a windstorm**, if there were trees involved. A big drop event can block a
system in one afternoon.

**Before listing a house.** Overflow staining on siding and a visibly loaded
gutter both read as deferred maintenance to a buyer, and both are cheap to
resolve.

## What we look at while we are up there

Not an inspection, and we are careful to say so. But someone at gutter height
with their hands in the system sees things, and we would rather tell you:

- **Spikes or hangers pulling out** of the fascia
- **Seams separating** at the corners and joins
- **Sagging runs** holding standing water, which means the fall is wrong
- **Rust or perforation** in older steel gutters
- **Soft or discoloured fascia** behind the gutter line
- **Moss and debris on the roof itself**, which is where the gutter debris is
  coming from and which is a [roof problem](/services/roof-cleaning/) rather than
  a gutter one
- **Downspouts discharging against the foundation** rather than away from it —
  often just a missing extension, and one of the cheapest fixes on any house

## What changes the price

Quoted on site. The things that move it:

1. **Linear feet of gutter**, which is the base — a rambler with a simple
   roofline is a very different number from a house with dormers, valleys and
   three separate roof planes.
2. **Storeys and access.** Single-storey on level ground is straightforward.
   Two-storey over a deck, a slope, or mature planting is slower and needs more
   equipment.
3. **Roof pitch.** A steep roof changes how the gutter is reached and, on some
   houses, rules out working from the roof entirely.
4. **How long it has been.** A system cleared last spring is a maintenance visit.
   A system with three years of matted needles, silt and moss is a different job,
   and there is more of it to carry away.
5. **Downspout count and complexity.** Long runs, multiple elbows and buried
   connections all take longer to clear and test properly.
6. **Guards.** Removing and refitting guards adds real time, and on some
   proprietary systems the clips are fragile enough to be worth quoting
   separately.

**Gutter brightening is priced separately** from clearing, because it is a
different process on a different surface — the outside face rather than the
inside channel.

## Access and safety, and why it is a real question

Gutter work is roof-height work, done in the wettest months of the year, on
surfaces that are slick with exactly the organic material being cleared. It is
the part of exterior maintenance most likely to injure whoever attempts it.

The failure mode is almost always the same: a ladder footed on soft wet ground,
or leaned against the gutter itself — which is a thin aluminium channel fixed
into a board that may already be rotten. Gutters are not structural. They are not
designed to take a ladder's load, and the ones most likely to fail under it are
the ones most in need of clearing.

We foot ladders properly, use standoffs so the load goes onto the roof edge or
the wall rather than the gutter, and work off the roof only where the pitch and
the surface make that sensible. Where a run genuinely cannot be reached safely,
we say so and quote for what can be done, rather than improvising at height on
someone else's property.

Insurance on work at this height is not a formality — an
uninsured fall on your property becomes your problem in a way most homeowners
have never had cause to think about.

## The connected problems

Gutters sit in the middle of the exterior system, which is why this job so often
turns up work that is really something else.

If the gutter fills faster than it should, look at the roof — [moss and
debris](/services/moss-removal/) shedding off the roof is what fills gutters, and
clearing the gutter without addressing the source means doing it again sooner.

If the siding under the gutter is stained or green, that is overflow that has
been happening for a while, and [house washing](/services/house-washing/) deals
with the mark once the cause is fixed.

If the water is getting away from the gutter but pooling on the path below,
that is drainage, and it is worth knowing before winter rather than during it.

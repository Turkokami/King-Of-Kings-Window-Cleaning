Solar in the Pacific Northwest is a longer game than solar in Arizona, and the
cleaning conversation is different too. There is enough rain here to do part of
the job for free, which means the honest answer to "do my panels need cleaning?"
is sometimes no — and we would rather say so than sell an annual visit to
somebody whose array is doing fine.

But rain is not a cleaner. It is a rinse, and there are specific things it does
not remove. Those are what this service is for.

## What rain does not wash off

**The edge band.** This is the important one and it is the reason most arrays
here benefit from cleaning at all. A framed panel has a raised aluminium lip
around its perimeter. On a tilted panel, water running down the glass reaches
that bottom lip and pools against it before it can escape. Silt, pollen and fine
debris carried in the water settle out exactly there and dry into a band along
the lower edge of every panel in the array.

It is easy to see once you know to look for it, and it does more damage to output
than its size suggests, for a reason worth understanding: a solar panel's cells
are wired in series. Current through a series string is limited by its weakest
element. Shade or soil across the bottom of the cells nearest the frame does not
cost you the fraction of the surface it covers — it can hold back the whole
string. This is why a thin dirty stripe can matter more than a large area of even
haze.

**Conifer pollen.** Spring in this region delivers an enormous pollen load,
predominantly from Douglas fir, and it arrives as a fine yellow-green film that
sticks. Rain wets it and redeposits it rather than removing it, and it is at its
worst in exactly the months when the array is starting to produce real output
again.

**Bird droppings.** Opaque, adherent, and unaffected by rain. A single well-placed
dropping over a cell can behave like the shading described above.

**Moss, lichen and organic growth.** Establishes at the frame edges and
underneath the array, where it is permanently shaded and damp. This is a
Whatcom County problem specifically, and the array is a good habitat for it.

**Needle and leaf debris**, particularly where it lodges between panels or under
the array on a low-tilt mount, and stays there holding moisture.

**Industrial and agricultural deposition.** Near the Cherry Point corridor, and
out on the farm ground through the Nooksack and Skagit valleys, the deposited
layer is finer and more adherent than ordinary dust, and rain does not lift it.

**Salt film.** At the marine edge in Blaine, Birch Bay, Anacortes and on the
islands, salt aerosol leaves a hygroscopic layer that stays slightly damp and
collects everything else that lands on it.

## Why deionized water, and nothing else

The method is not incidental. It is the whole service, and it is worth
understanding why the obvious alternatives are wrong.

A modern solar panel is not simply a sheet of glass. The front surface carries an
**anti-reflective coating** whose entire job is to get more light through the
glass and into the cells rather than bouncing it back off. That coating is
microscopically thin and it is the most valuable and most vulnerable surface on
the panel.

**Detergents** leave residue. Any surfactant that helps a solution wet the glass
also leaves a film when it dries, and that film is slightly tacky — it attracts
and holds the next round of dust rather than shedding it, so the panel soils
faster after cleaning than before. Some cleaning chemistries also attack
anti-reflective coatings over repeated applications.

**Abrasives** — including scouring pads, stiff brushes, and any grit picked up
and dragged across the surface — cause micro-scratching. Scratches scatter
incoming light instead of transmitting it. The loss is small per scratch,
permanent, and cumulative.

**Ordinary tap or well water** carries dissolved minerals. When it evaporates on
the glass, those minerals stay behind as spotting — the same hard-water deposit
described on the [window cleaning](/services/window-cleaning/) page, on a surface
where optical clarity is the entire point.

**High pressure** can force water past the panel's edge seals and into the
laminate, which is a failure mode that ends a panel's life.

Deionized water solves all of it at once. With the minerals stripped out, water
becomes an aggressive solvent in a mild way — it actively wants to pick up
dissolved material and carry it off, and then it evaporates leaving **nothing
behind at all**. No residue, no spotting, no film. Combined with a soft brush on
a pole and no meaningful pressure, it cleans the glass without ever touching the
coating with anything harder than water and bristle.

That is the method, and it is the same pure-water approach used on the upper
glass of a house — applied to a surface where the stakes are higher.

## Why we never walk on panels

Standing on a solar panel is the single most damaging thing a person can do to
one, and it is done routinely by people who do not know better.

The damage is invisible. Body weight on the glass flexes the laminate and causes
**micro-cracks in the silicon cells** underneath. The panel looks unchanged. It
continues producing. But the cracked cells degrade faster than they should,
output falls progressively over the following years, and the failure is
attributed to age rather than to whoever stood on it. Every major manufacturer
treats foot traffic as abuse and excludes it from warranty coverage.

We clean from the ground with extension equipment on the majority of residential
arrays. Where the roof has to be accessed, we work on the roof deck around the
array rather than on it — which is also why array layout and roof access get
looked at during the quote rather than discovered on the day.

## The electrical reality

Worth being plain about, because it changes what we will and will not touch.

A solar array is a live direct-current electrical system, and **it cannot be
turned off by a switch**. As long as daylight is falling on the panels, they are
generating. The inverter can be shut down; the panels and the DC wiring between
them remain energised. DC arcing is also less forgiving than AC.

Practically, that means we clean the glass and we do not go near anything
electrical. Damaged or cracked panels, delamination, discoloured cells, scorching
or browning at a junction box, chafed or sagging wiring, corroded connectors,
loose mounting hardware — all of these get photographed and reported to you, and
referred to your installer or a licensed electrician. We do not open, disconnect,
adjust or repair any part of a solar system, and any contractor offering to is
outside their lane.

## What the visit involves

- **Assessment** — array size, mounting, tilt, access, condition, and whether
  cleaning is actually warranted
- **A pre-clean output note**, where the system's monitoring makes that available
- **Deionized water clean** with a soft brush on a pole, at low pressure
- **Particular attention to the bottom edge band**, for the reasons above
- **Frames and the gaps between panels** cleared of lodged debris
- **Moss and growth cleared** from around and beneath the array where reachable
- **Condition report** on anything visible, with photographs
- **No foot traffic on the panels**, ever

## When to book

**Late spring** is the single best slot in this region, once the Douglas fir
pollen has finished. Cleaning before the pollen has stopped falling means
cleaning again.

**Early autumn**, particularly on the agricultural flats after harvest dust and
before the array goes into the low-light months, when every percentage point of
the little available production matters more.

**After a construction project** nearby, or after a windstorm that brought debris
down onto the array.

**When the numbers say so** — see below.

**Not on a hot panel in full sun.** Cold water on hot glass is a thermal shock
risk, and panels in direct summer sun get considerably hotter than the air.
Morning, evening, or an overcast day is correct, which in this region is rarely a
scheduling problem.

## Let the monitoring tell you

This is the part that makes solar different from every other surface we clean:
your array measures its own performance, and you can check whether cleaning is
warranted rather than guessing.

Most systems installed here have monitoring — an app or a portal showing daily
and monthly production. The useful comparison is **the same month against
previous years**, not against last month, because seasonal variation swamps
everything else. A clear-sky day in May this year against a clear-sky day in May
last year tells you something. May against March tells you nothing.

If production on comparable days has drifted down a few percent and the panels
look filmed or banded, cleaning will likely recover most of it. If production is
holding steady year on year, your array is fine and we will say so.

A steep or sudden drop is a different matter and is usually not soiling. That is
an inverter, a string, or a panel fault, and it needs your installer rather than
a bucket.

## Roof-mounted and ground-mounted

**Roof-mounted** is the majority here. The considerations are access, roof pitch,
and the gap between the array and the roof surface — that gap is a shaded,
sheltered, permanently damp space, which makes it excellent habitat for moss and
a collection point for needles. It is also where rodents and birds nest if the
perimeter is not screened.

Roof arrays interact with the rest of the roof, too: an array sitting above a
mossy roof section will keep collecting what grows below it, which is a
[roof cleaning](/services/roof-cleaning/) and
[moss removal](/services/moss-removal/) question as much as a panel one.

**Ground-mounted** arrays are simpler, safer and cheaper to clean, and they have
their own soiling profile: mud splash in heavy rain, grass clippings from mowing,
dust from a nearby drive, and vegetation growing up into the lower edge. The
bottom row is always the dirty one.

## What changes the price

1. **Number of panels.** The base figure.
2. **Roof or ground mount**, and how the array is reached.
3. **Roof pitch and array position** — a low-tilt array high on a steep roof is a
   different proposition from one at the eaves.
4. **Condition.** A maintained array on an annual cycle is quick. An array that
   has never been cleaned, with moss established at the frames, is not.
5. **Growth under and around the array**, if it needs clearing.
6. **Water access on site**, since deionized water is produced from a supply.

Combining it with other work on the same visit is usually cheaper, because the
access and set-up are shared.

## The honest position on payback

We would rather set the expectation properly than oversell this.

In a high-dust, low-rain climate, panel cleaning has an obvious and rapid
payback. Here, rain does a meaningful share of the work, so the return is real
but more modest, and it is concentrated in the specific soiling types listed at
the top of this page — the edge band, pollen film, growth and droppings — rather
than in general dust.

Where cleaning clearly pays for itself: arrays under trees, arrays near the
industrial or agricultural corridors, arrays at the marine edge, arrays with
visible growth at the frames, and any array that has never been cleaned.

Where it may not, yet: a newer, well-sited, unshaded array on an exposed roof,
two or three years old, with monitoring showing steady year-on-year production.

Send a photograph and a screenshot of your monitoring before booking and we will
give you a straight answer, including "not this year" if that is the answer.

## Common mistakes worth avoiding

Four things people do to their own arrays that cost more than the cleaning would
have.

**Standing on them.** Covered above, and it is worth repeating because it is the
most common and the most expensive.

**Using a pressure washer.** Force at a panel edge can breach the seal between
glass and frame and let moisture into the laminate. That ends a panel.

**Cleaning with a hose and a household cleaner on a sunny afternoon.** Three
mistakes at once: mineral spotting from the tap water, residue from the cleaner,
and thermal shock on hot glass.

**Scraping something off with a blade.** Bird droppings and tree sap tempt people
towards a scraper. The anti-reflective coating does not survive it.

If you want to do it yourself, the safe version is: overcast day or early
morning, cool panels, soft brush, and the purest water you can get, from the
ground, never from on top of the array.

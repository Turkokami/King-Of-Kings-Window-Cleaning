A roof in this climate spends most of the year damp and a good part of it in
shade. That combination grows things. What grows, how much damage it does, and
what can safely be done about it depends almost entirely on what the roof is made
of — which is why "roof cleaning" is not one service and should not be quoted as
one.

The single most important thing on this page: **a pressure washer does not belong
on a roof.** Everything else follows from that.

## Why pressure washing a roof is a bad idea

It is worth being specific, because pressure washing roofs is still sold in this
region and it still damages roofs.

An asphalt composition shingle — which is what most houses here have — is a mat
saturated with asphalt and surfaced with a layer of mineral granules. Those
granules are not decoration. They are the shingle's UV protection and its
weathering surface, and the asphalt underneath degrades quickly once they are
gone. A shingle sheds granules slowly over its whole life; that is normal. A
pressure washer removes years of that in seconds, and the loss is not recoverable.

High pressure also drives water **upward, under the shingle courses**, into the
layer that is designed to shed water flowing down. Water that gets under a course
does not necessarily leak immediately — it soaks the underlayment and the deck,
and the consequence arrives later as rot rather than as a drip.

And it voids warranties. Roofing manufacturers are explicit that high-pressure
cleaning is not an approved maintenance method. Damage from it is not covered,
and on a roof still within its warranty period that is a meaningful thing to
throw away for a faster job.

If a quote for roof cleaning is notably cheap and notably fast, ask what pressure
is being used. There is usually a reason.

## What soft washing actually is

Soft washing replaces mechanical force with chemistry and time.

A cleaning solution appropriate to the roofing material is applied at low
pressure — comparable to a garden hose — and left to dwell long enough to kill the
organism. The roof is then rinsed gently. The dead growth releases over the
following weeks under normal rain and wind, which is why a soft-washed roof
continues to improve for a month or two after the visit rather than looking
finished the moment the truck leaves.

That delay is the part homeowners are least prepared for, so it is worth saying
plainly: **an honest soft wash does not look completely finished on the day.**
Black algae staining lifts immediately and dramatically. Thick moss does not — it
dies, goes brown and brittle, and weathers off. Anyone getting all of the moss
off a roof on the day is scraping or blasting it, and both of those cost you
roof.

Careful operators pre-wet planting below the work area, cover what is sensitive,
manage where runoff goes, and rinse everything down afterwards. In the Lake
Whatcom watershed — Sudden Valley, Geneva and the surrounding catchment — runoff
management is not just good practice, it is a genuine constraint, and the method
has to reflect that.

## The three things growing on your roof

They look similar from the ground and they are biologically unrelated. Treatment
differs.

**Algae** — the black streaks. Almost always *Gloeocapsa magma*, a cyanobacteria
that arrived on the roof as airborne spores and stayed because the surface was
damp enough. It feeds partly on the limestone filler used in asphalt shingles,
which is why it is so specifically a composition-roof problem. It spreads
downward with water flow, producing the characteristic vertical streaking that
starts near the ridge. It holds moisture against the shingle and darkens the
surface, which raises roof temperature — a real if modest energy cost. More on
black algae streaks.

**Lichen** — the flat, crusty, pale green or grey patches. A symbiotic
organism, part fungus and part alga, and the most tenacious of the three. Lichen
does not sit on the surface; it anchors *into* it, and removing established
lichen from asphalt takes granules with it no matter how carefully it is done.
This is the growth where the honest answer is often "we will kill it and let it
weather off over a year", not "we will remove it today".

**Moss** — the green cushions, thickest at the shaded eaves and in the valleys.
Structurally the most damaging of the three, and different enough to have its own
service: see [roof moss removal](/services/moss-removal/).

## Roof by roof

**Composition asphalt shingle** — the majority of roofs in both counties. Soft
wash only, solution chosen for the growth present, no walking on the roof in hot
weather and careful footfall in cold. The granule question above governs
everything.

**Cedar shake and shingle** — common on older Bellingham housing and on
architect-designed homes around Chuckanut, Edgemoor and the lake. Wood is
absorbent and living, so the solution has to be gentler and saturation controlled.
Cedar also holds moss deep in the checking and between the courses, and clearing
it aggressively splits shakes. Expectations matter here: weathered cedar cleans
up considerably but it does not return to new cedar colour, and any contractor
implying otherwise is setting up a disappointment. More on
moss on cedar shake.

**Standing seam and corrugated metal** — increasingly common on new builds and
outbuildings throughout the county. Metal does not grow algae the way asphalt
does, but it collects organic debris in the seams and it streaks. The
consideration is the coating: the factory finish is what makes the roof last, and
the wrong chemistry dulls or strips it. Metal is soft washed with a solution
chosen for the coating, never scoured. More on
[metal roof soft washing](/services/metal-roof-soft-washing/).

**Concrete and clay tile** — a smaller share of the housing stock here, mostly on
newer builds. Tile is durable but brittle underfoot and the traffic risk is
greater than the cleaning risk. Access planning matters more than solution choice.

**Torch-down and low-slope membrane** — flats, additions, garages and most
commercial buildings. Different problem entirely: standing water and debris
rather than growth, and the seams are the vulnerability. Cleaning is gentle and
the useful part of the visit is usually clearing the drains.

## What roof growth actually costs you

Not aesthetics, or not only aesthetics.

**Moisture retention.** Everything growing on a roof holds water against the
surface, which extends the time the roof spends wet in a climate where that is
already most of the year.

**Granule loss.** Lichen anchored into the surface takes granules when it goes,
and algae accelerates the breakdown of the filler in the shingle itself.

**Freeze damage.** Water held in growth at the eaves, then frozen, expands.
Relevant across the whole service area and acute in the foothills.

**Gutter load.** Growth and debris shedding off the roof is what fills the
gutters, which is why these two jobs belong together.

**Heat.** A dark, algae-covered roof runs hotter than a clean one — modest, but
real, and it works against the attic ventilation.

**Resale.** A streaked roof reads as an old roof to a buyer, whether or not it is,
and it is one of the first things a home inspector photographs.

## When to book

**Spring**, once the weather has settled enough for a solution to dwell rather
than being rinsed off by the next shower, and before the summer growth season.

**Early autumn**, which in this climate is arguably the better slot — treating
before the wet season means the roof goes into winter with the growth dead
rather than feeding on six months of rain.

**Not in a freeze**, and not on a hot roof in full summer sun, when solution
evaporates before it can work and shingle surfaces are soft underfoot.

**On a cycle of three to five years** for most roofs here. Under heavy conifer
cover or in the foothills, closer to three. On an exposed roof on the flats,
closer to five.

## What to expect after the visit

Setting this out because it is where most dissatisfaction with honest roof
cleaning comes from — not from the work, but from expecting the wrong timeline.

**On the day.** Black algae streaking will look dramatically better, often almost
entirely gone. Thick moss will look wet and darkened but will still be there.
Lichen will look largely unchanged.

**Two to four weeks.** Moss browns, dries and becomes brittle. It begins shedding
in wind and rain. Gutters may collect noticeably more debris during this period,
which is normal and is why gutter clearing is usually scheduled after roof work
rather than before.

**Two to three months.** Most of the dead moss has weathered off. The roof looks
substantially different from how it looked on the day the work was done.

**Six to twelve months.** Lichen, which is the slowest, has largely released.

This is what "let the weather do the removal" means in practice. The alternative
— getting it all off on the day — requires scraping or pressure, and both cost
roof life. We would rather explain the timeline than deliver a faster result that
does damage.

## What changes the price

Quoted on site, and the drivers are different from most exterior work:

1. **Roof area and number of planes.** A simple gable is straightforward; valleys,
   dormers, multiple levels and complex geometry all add time.
2. **Pitch.** A steep roof changes the whole method and may mean the work is done
   entirely from ladders and poles rather than from the surface.
3. **Roofing material.** Asphalt, cedar, metal and tile each need different
   solution, different pressure, different care.
4. **What is growing and how much.** Light algae streaking is a straightforward
   treatment. Deep moss cushions in the valleys and established lichen are a
   heavier job.
5. **Access and what is below.** Mature planting, water features, a pool, or
   neighbouring property close to the drip line all mean more protection work and
   more careful runoff management.
6. **Catchment sensitivity.** In the Lake Whatcom watershed the method is more
   constrained, and constrained methods take longer.

## Working safely on a roof

Roof work is the highest-risk thing we do and the risk is not evenly distributed
across the year. A wet, mossy, north-facing composition roof in February is one
of the more genuinely dangerous working surfaces in residential trades — moss is
slick, and the pitch that looks walkable from the ground frequently is not.

Our default is to work **off the roof rather than on it** wherever the method
allows: soft washing is applied at low pressure and can be delivered from ladders
and extension equipment on a great many houses without anyone standing on the
surface at all. That is better for the operator and better for the roof, because
foot traffic on asphalt shingle causes granule loss in its own right — especially
on a cold roof, where shingles are brittle, and on a hot one, where they are soft.

Where the roof does have to be walked, it is walked with the right footwear, the
right anchor arrangement and an assessment made on the day rather than assumed
from the quote. If a roof is not safe to work on in the conditions on the day, we
reschedule. That is not us being precious about it.

## Where roof cleaning stops and roofing begins

We clean roofs. We do not repair them, and we are careful about the line.

We do not replace shingles, reseal flashings, repair valleys or ridge caps,
address leaks, or carry out roof inspections or condition reports. If we can see
something — lifted shingles, exposed nail heads, damaged flashing, deteriorated
ridge — we will tell you, as an observation from someone who was standing on the
roof. That is not an assessment and it is not a substitute for a roofer.

We will also tell you when a roof should not be cleaned. A roof near the end of
its service life, with widespread granule loss, curling or brittle shingles, can
be damaged by any cleaning method, and cleaning it does not extend it in a way
that justifies the spend. That conversation happens at the quote.

## The right order of work

If several things need doing, sequence matters, and getting it wrong means paying
twice:

1. **Roof first.** It is the highest surface, and everything that comes off it
   lands on everything below.
2. **Gutters second** — [gutter cleaning](/services/gutter-cleaning/) — because
   roof work puts material into them.
3. **Siding third** — [house washing](/services/house-washing/) — to take off what
   the first two steps deposited, plus the staining underneath.
4. **Windows last** — [window cleaning](/services/window-cleaning/) — because glass
   is the surface that shows a mistake made above it.

Booking them together is generally cheaper and always less disruptive than four
separate visits, because access is set up once.

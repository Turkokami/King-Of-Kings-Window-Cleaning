Pressure washing is the service most likely to be sold as one thing and delivered
as another. The machine is capable of stripping paint off wood and etching a
permanent pattern into concrete, and the difference between a good result and
expensive damage is almost entirely about knowing when *not* to use the pressure
it is capable of.

So the first thing worth stating is the boundary. In this business, pressure
belongs on **hard horizontal surfaces**: concrete, pavers, brick, stone. It does
not belong on siding, and it certainly does not belong on a roof. Those get a
soft wash instead, and there are separate pages for
[house washing](/services/house-washing/) and
[roof cleaning](/services/roof-cleaning/).

## What we actually pressure wash

**Driveways and parking areas.** Concrete, asphalt, pavers and exposed
aggregate. Usually the largest single surface on a property and the one where
the before-and-after is most dramatic.

**Walkways, paths and steps.** The highest-value work we do, and not for cosmetic
reasons — see the section on slip risk below.

**Patios and pool surrounds.** Concrete, pavers, flagstone, stamped concrete.

**Decks.** Softwood, hardwood and composite, each at very different pressures.

**Fences.** Cedar and treated softwood, generally to remove the grey-green
biological film rather than to strip them.

**Retaining walls, steps and hardscape.** Block, stone and concrete.

**Garbage and recycling areas, and commercial hardstanding.** Unglamorous and
frequently the reason a commercial client calls in the first place.

## The slip hazard nobody prices properly

Of everything on this page, this is the part worth taking seriously.

For roughly eight months of the year, exterior concrete in Whatcom and Skagit
County is damp. Damp concrete in shade grows a biological film — algae mostly,
with moss establishing in the joints and along the edges. That film is
imperceptibly slippery when damp and genuinely dangerous when wet.

The places it matters are the places people use every day without thinking:
the front steps, the path from the driveway to the door, a sloped walkway down
the side of the house, the back step to the garden. These are exactly the
surfaces that stay shaded, and exactly the surfaces someone walks on carrying
groceries in the rain in November.

A slip on a wet mossy step is one of the more common ways people get seriously
hurt at home, and older residents are disproportionately affected. Clearing that
film is among the cheapest risk reductions available on a property, and it is why
we will suggest doing the steps and paths first if a budget has to be prioritised
— ahead of the driveway, which is usually what the homeowner rang about.

## Surfaces, and what each one can take

The pressure is not a setting on the machine so much as a decision about the
material in front of you.

**Concrete** takes the most, and still has a limit. Overdone, the cement paste at
the surface erodes and exposes the aggregate beneath, leaving a rougher, more
porous surface that soils faster and grows more. Worse, an operator working with
a narrow nozzle rather than a surface cleaner leaves **wand marks** — visible
arcs and stripes where the passes overlapped unevenly. Those are permanent, and
they are the single most common complaint about cheap driveway cleaning. A flat
surface cleaner, which encloses two rotating jets under a shroud, produces an
even result and is the correct tool for slabs.

**Pavers and segmental block** clean well, but the pressure lifts the jointing
sand out of the gaps. That sand is structural — it is what stops pavers rocking
and shifting — so a proper paver clean includes re-sanding the joints afterward.
An operator who does not mention re-sanding has not thought about it.

**Brick** varies enormously by age. Modern brick is fine. Older, softer brick and
lime mortar can be eroded by pressure that concrete would shrug off, and once
mortar joints are opened up, water gets into the wall.

**Softwood decking** is the material most often ruined. Too much pressure raises
the wood grain, leaving a furred surface that feels rough underfoot, weathers
faster and takes stain unevenly forever after. Decks are cleaned at low pressure,
usually with a cleaning solution doing most of the work and the wand doing very
little.

**Composite decking** has its own manufacturer requirements, and exceeding them
voids warranties. Some composites should never see a pressure washer at all.

**Asphalt** softens in heat and erodes under high pressure. Cleaned gently, if at
all.

**Natural stone** depends entirely on the stone. Sandstone is soft; granite is
not. Judged case by case.

## Where the water goes

This gets less attention than it should, and in this county it genuinely matters.

Wash water from exterior cleaning carries whatever was on the surface — organic
material, detergent, and on driveways, oil and vehicle residue. That water is not
supposed to enter the storm drain system, which in most of this area discharges
to creeks, to the bay, or to the lake without treatment.

The constraint is sharpest in the **Lake Whatcom watershed** — Sudden Valley,
Geneva and the surrounding catchment — because the lake is the drinking-water
supply for a large part of the county, and phosphorus and sediment loading into
it is a live, long-running local issue with real regulation attached.

Practically, managing runoff means directing wash water onto landscaping and
vegetated ground where it can filter rather than into the nearest drain, blocking
or diverting inlets where necessary, containing and collecting on jobs where the
contamination warrants it, and using the mildest chemistry that will do the job.

Ask any pressure washing contractor where the water is going. If the answer is a
blank look, that tells you something about how the rest of the job will be done.

## What changes the price

1. **Area.** Square footage is the base for slab work.
2. **Surface and condition.** Light surface film is a fast job. Years of
   established moss, embedded lichen or heavy oil staining is not.
3. **Access.** Whether the machine and hoses can reach, whether there is a water
   supply, and how far the equipment has to be carried.
4. **Re-sanding**, on paver work.
5. **Runoff management.** Sites needing containment or diversion take longer.
6. **Sealing**, if you want it — a separate step, priced separately.
7. **Delicacy.** Old brick, natural stone and softwood decking take longer
   precisely because they are done gently.

## When to book

**Spring**, when winter has left a full season of biological growth and everybody
wants their outdoor space back. This is the busiest window.

**Autumn**, before the wet season, which is arguably the smarter slot for
anything that is a slip risk. Clearing the steps in October means the winter is
spent walking on clean concrete rather than on algae.

**Before an event**, before listing a property, or ahead of a photography
appointment. Clean hardscape changes how a property reads more than its cost
suggests.

**Not in a freeze.** Water on concrete in freezing conditions is a hazard to
create rather than remove, and equipment does not appreciate it either.

## About sealing

A sealer applied after cleaning extends the result on concrete and pavers, makes
subsequent cleaning easier, and reduces staining.

Two honest caveats. First, sealer is a maintenance item, not a permanent
treatment — it wears, particularly in vehicle tracks, and needs redoing on a
cycle. Second, some sealers make a surface more slippery when wet, which on steps
and sloped walkways is precisely the wrong outcome. Where slip resistance
matters, either an additive is used or the surface is left unsealed.

## The equipment, in plain terms

Two numbers describe a pressure washer, and the one that matters most is the one
nobody advertises.

**PSI** is pressure — the force at the nozzle. It gets all the marketing
attention and it is the number most capable of doing damage.

**GPM** is flow — gallons per minute. Flow is what actually carries dirt away,
and it is why a professional machine cleans a driveway several times faster than
a consumer unit with a similar PSI figure. Cleaning is mostly a function of
volume, not force. This is also why the professional answer to a stubborn surface
is rarely "turn the pressure up".

**Hot water** matters more than pressure on anything greasy. Heat does to oil
what force cannot, which is why commercial kitchen surrounds, refuse areas and
oil-stained driveways get hot water where a patio does not need it.

**The surface cleaner** — the round shrouded attachment with jets rotating
underneath — is what produces an even result on flat work. It holds a constant
distance from the surface and overlaps its own passes, which is exactly what a
hand-held wand cannot do. If someone is cleaning your driveway with a wand alone,
expect stripes.

**Nozzle choice** runs from a wide fan to a pencil-thin jet, plus the rotary
"turbo" nozzle that concentrates force into a spinning point. The turbo nozzle is
the one that etches concrete and destroys decking, and it has a narrow set of
legitimate uses.

## What the result actually looks like

Managing this in advance saves disappointment, because concrete is not uniform.

**Clean is not new.** Pressure washing removes what is on and in the surface. It
does not remove age. A twenty-year-old driveway comes out looking like a clean
twenty-year-old driveway, and the areas that have weathered differently will
still look different.

**Edges show.** If we clean a driveway and stop at the property line, the
boundary is going to be visible, because the adjacent surface is still carrying
years of film. Same for a patio that meets an uncleaned path. Worth deciding the
scope with that in mind.

**Some staining is permanent.** Deep oil, old rust, and anything that has
chemically altered the surface will lighten rather than disappear.

**Pavers move slightly.** Cleaning lifts jointing sand and the surface settles
minutely as it is re-sanded. Normal.

**It will grow back.** In this climate, on a shaded surface, one to three years.
That is not a failure of the clean; it is what living in a temperate rainforest
means.

## Different across the service area

Hard-surface growth tracks shade and rainfall closely, so the maintenance cycle
varies as much as it does on roofs.

In the **foothills and along the Mount Baker corridor** — Deming, Acme, Van
Zandt, Kendall, Maple Falls, Glacier — high rainfall and dense conifer cover mean
paths and driveways green over quickly, and tannin staining from needle drop is
routine. Annual cleaning is realistic here.

Around **Lake Whatcom**, in Sudden Valley and Geneva, the same heavy shade
applies, with the watershed constraint on how the work is done.

In **Bellingham**, it varies by neighbourhood. Mature-tree areas — Edgemoor,
Fairhaven, Silver Beach, Whatcom Falls, Columbia — hold damp far longer than the
open newer developments at Cordata, Barkley or the Guide Meridian corridor.

On the **agricultural flats** through Lynden, Everson, Mount Vernon and
Burlington, surfaces are more exposed and dry faster, so biological growth is
slower — but field dust and farm traffic put a different kind of soiling on
driveways and hardstanding.

At the **marine edge** — Blaine, Birch Bay, Semiahmoo, Anacortes and the islands
— salt is the additional factor, and it is hard on metal fixings, railings and
anything with a coating on it.

## For landlords, HOAs and property managers

Hard surfaces are where the liability sits on a managed property, and it is worth
being blunt about that.

Shared walkways, entrance steps, communal patios and parking areas are used by
people who did not choose them and did not inspect them. A slip on a mossy
communal step is a claim, and "it was on the maintenance list" is a much better
position than "nobody had looked at it". A scheduled autumn clean of the
circulation routes — the paths people actually walk, not the decorative areas —
is inexpensive relative to what it protects against.

Turnover cleans between tenancies are the other common request, usually alongside
[window cleaning](/services/window-cleaning/), and they run on the tight
scheduling window between one tenant leaving and the next arriving.

## What we do not do

- We do not pressure wash siding, stucco or roofs. Those are soft-wash surfaces.
- We do not strip or refinish decks, apply stain, or do carpentry.
- We do not repair, resurface or replace concrete, or repoint brickwork.
- We do not clean anything where the pressure required would exceed what the
  material can take — we will tell you at the quote rather than discovering it
  on the surface.

## The connected work

Pressure washing is usually one part of a wider exterior clean, and sequencing
matters. Hard surfaces come **last**, after the roof, the gutters and the siding,
because everything above sheds onto everything below and doing the driveway first
means doing it twice.

If your paths are green, look at what is shading them. If your driveway stains in
one particular spot every winter, look at the downspout above it —
[gutter](/services/gutter-cleaning/) discharge landing on a slab is a common and
easily fixed cause. And if the siding above a patio is dirty, it is worth doing
[house washing](/services/house-washing/) in the same visit while the equipment
and the access are already set up.

## Getting the sequence right on a whole-property clean

When several things are booked together — which is common, and cheaper than
separate visits — the order is not arbitrary:

1. **Roof**, because it is highest and everything comes off it downward.
2. **Gutters**, which catch what the roof work releases.
3. **Siding**, taking off what the first two deposited plus its own growth.
4. **Windows**, because glass shows any mistake made above it.
5. **Hard surfaces last** — driveway, paths, patio, deck — because every stage
   above rinses material onto the ground.

A contractor who cleans the driveway first is going to be cleaning it again, or
leaving you with a driveway that looks worse at the end of the day than it did at
the start.

## What each surface needs

| Surface | Method | What to expect |
|---|---|---|
| Broom-finish concrete drive or path | Surface cleaner at working pressure, solution first where growth is present | The dramatic before-and-after line; grip restored |
| Exposed aggregate, stamped or coloured concrete | Lower pressure, more dwell | Clean, with the finish intact — no etching, no striping |
| Pavers with sand joints | Lower pressure, joint sand checked afterwards | Sand loss is the risk, not the stone |
| Brick and older lime mortar | Gentle only | Joints erode under pressure; we will say where we stop |
| Composite and timber decking | Soft wash | Force furrows timber and hazes composite |

Runoff is managed and planting is pre-wetted and rinsed. Where a slab is spalling
or has lost its cream layer, cleaning improves it and does not resurface it.

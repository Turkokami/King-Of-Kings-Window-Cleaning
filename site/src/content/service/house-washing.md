The exterior walls of a house in this region spend most of the year damp. What
grows on them is not really dirt — it is biology, it is concentrated on the sides
that get the least sun, and it comes off easily if you use the right method and
does considerable damage if you use the wrong one.

The wrong method is a pressure washer. That statement is the reason this is a
separate service from [pressure washing](/services/pressure-washing/), and it is
worth understanding why rather than taking it on trust.

## Why siding must not be pressure washed

Siding is built on the same principle as a shingle roof: overlapping horizontal
courses, each lapping the one below, so water running down the wall stays on the
outside. Vinyl, fiber cement, wood lap, cedar shingle — all of them work this
way, and all of them are designed against water travelling *downward*.

A pressure washer aimed at a wall drives water **upward and sideways, behind the
laps**. It goes into the gap the siding relies on being a one-way path, past the
weather-resistive barrier if there is a gap in it, and into the wall assembly.
It also gets in around window and door flashings, which are detailed for
gravity-driven water, not for a jet.

Water in a wall cavity in a climate this wet does not dry out quickly. It causes
the failures that show up years later as soft sheathing, mould inside the wall,
and rot at the sill.

Beyond the water intrusion, force does direct damage: it cracks and permanently
etches vinyl, chips and erodes fiber cement, blasts paint off painted wood,
splinters and furs cedar, and drives water into stucco. On older vinyl in
particular — which is brittle from UV exposure — a pressure washer can crack a
panel outright.

**Soft washing** does the job instead: pressure at roughly garden-hose level,
with a cleaning solution formulated for the growth and the substrate doing the
actual work. It is applied, given time to dwell and kill the organism, then
rinsed. Slower to look impressive and far better for the building.

## What is actually growing on your walls

**Algae** — the green film, heaviest on north and shaded elevations. The single
most common thing we remove here. It sits on the surface, feeds on moisture and
airborne organic matter, and washes off cleanly once killed.

**Mildew and mould** — the black speckling and blotching, again concentrated in
shade and in the areas that stay damp longest. Frequently found behind shrubs
planted too close to the wall.

**Moss and lichen** — on the lower courses, in the seams, on north-facing cedar
shingle, and anywhere splash keeps a surface permanently wet.

**Oxidation and chalking** — not growth at all. UV degrades the surface of vinyl
and painted siding over years, leaving a chalky layer that rubs off on your hand.
Washing removes the loose chalk, which usually improves appearance considerably,
but it does not restore the finish. Badly oxidised vinyl that has gone matte and
faded is not going to come back to gloss, and any contractor implying otherwise
is setting you up to be disappointed.

**Overflow staining** — the vertical dirty streaking below a gutter that has been
overflowing. It cleans off, but it will come back until the
[gutter](/services/gutter-cleaning/) is dealt with, and we will say so.

**Cobwebs, wasp and mud-dauber nests, and general debris** in the soffits and
under the eaves — cleared as part of the job.

**Industrial and agricultural film** — near the Cherry Point corridor and out on
the farm ground, a fine deposited layer that is neither growth nor ordinary dirt
and needs detergent rather than force.

## Protecting what is around the house

This is the part of house washing that separates careful operators from fast
ones, and it is worth knowing what should be happening.

**Planting gets pre-wetted** before any solution is applied. A plant saturated
with clean water absorbs far less of anything that lands on it afterwards, and
this single step prevents most plant damage.

**Sensitive planting gets covered.** Newly planted material, anything unusual and
anything under the immediate work area.

**Everything gets rinsed** at the end, generously.

**Runoff is directed** away from where it should not go. As with all our exterior
work, the constraint is sharpest in the **Lake Whatcom watershed** — Sudden
Valley, Geneva and the surrounding catchment — where the lake supplies drinking
water and what enters it is a live regulatory issue.

**Electrical is checked** — exterior outlets, light fixtures, meter bases, and
anything with a compromised cover. Water and electricity at a wall is a real
consideration and it is checked before rather than discovered during.

**Air conditioning and heat pump units** get covered or avoided.

Ask any house washing contractor how they handle planting. The answer tells you
most of what you need to know about how the rest of the job will go.

## When to book

**Spring** is the busiest window, and for good reason — a winter of damp has left
the shaded elevations green, and everybody notices it the first sunny week.

**Autumn** works well too and is less contended, and going into the wet season
with clean walls means the growth starts from nothing rather than from an
established base.

**Before painting.** Washing is the prep. Paint applied over a chalked, algae-
covered surface does not bond properly and fails early, and any painter worth
hiring will insist the walls are washed first.

**Before listing a property.** A clean elevation photographs dramatically better,
and a green north wall reads as neglect in a way that is out of proportion to
what it actually means.

**Not in a freeze**, and not on a wall in full direct summer sun, where solution
evaporates before it can work.

**Every two to four years** for most houses here, more often on heavily shaded
elevations.

## The one-elevation option

Worth knowing, because it saves people money.

Growth on a house is rarely evenly distributed. The north side is green; the
south side is fine. If the budget is tight, washing the shaded elevations only is
a completely legitimate approach, and it addresses the part that actually looks
bad and holds moisture.

We will quote it either way and tell you honestly whether the sunny elevations
need doing at all this cycle. Selling a whole-house wash to somebody who needs
one wall is easy and we would rather not.

## What house washing will and will not change

Setting expectations properly, because siding is a surface where several
different things get called "dirty" and only some of them wash off.

**Comes off completely:** algae, mildew, mould, moss, lichen, cobwebs, wasp and
mud-dauber nests, general atmospheric dirt, and most overflow staining.

**Improves substantially:** chalked and oxidised surfaces, which lose the loose
powdery layer and look markedly better without being restored; weathered cedar,
which cleans up but stays weathered; and long-standing organic staining, which
lightens.

**Does not change:** fading. UV degradation of the pigment in vinyl or paint is
a change to the material, not a layer on top of it. A wall that has gone from
deep green to pale sage is not dirty. Nor does washing repair cracks, close gaps,
restore failed caulking, or do anything about damage under the siding.

The useful test at the quote is simple: if a wet finger on the surface comes away
green or grey, it is coming off. If the wall looks the same colour wet as dry,
that is the material and washing will not alter it.

## Rentals, HOAs and multi-unit buildings

A large share of this work is managed property rather than owner-occupied, and
the drivers are different.

**Turnover cleans** between tenancies, usually bundled with
[window cleaning](/services/window-cleaning/) and scheduled into the tight window
between one tenant leaving and the next arriving.

**HOA and condo elevations**, where the association owns the exterior and the
appearance of the building is a standing agenda item. On multi-building sites the
efficient approach is one mobilisation across the whole community — access and
equipment are set up once, and the buildings end up matching, which is usually
the board's actual concern.

**Vacation rentals**, where the exterior is the first photograph a guest sees and
the review economy is unforgiving about a green wall.

**Pre-sale washes** on properties coming to market, generally alongside
[pressure washing](/services/pressure-washing/) of the driveway and paths.

For managed properties the useful conversation is about scheduling rather than
individual jobs — an annual or biennial cycle on the shaded elevations costs less
and looks better than reacting when someone complains.

## What changes the price

1. **House size and number of storeys.** Square footage of wall, and how much of
   it is above reach.
2. **Siding material.** Cedar, stucco and failing paint all take longer than
   vinyl because they are done more gently.
3. **Condition.** Light film is quick. Heavy moss, lichen and years of growth are
   not.
4. **Access.** Tight side yards, steep ground, mature planting against the wall,
   decking and outbuildings all slow the work.
5. **Landscaping.** More protection work means more time.
6. **Detail.** Shutters, extensive trim, multiple dormers and decorative elements
   add real hours.

## What is in the solution

Homeowners are entitled to ask, and on a wall it matters more than on a driveway
because the runoff lands in the beds directly beneath it.

A soft wash solution is typically a dilute cleaner with a surfactant that helps
it cling to a vertical surface long enough to work — which is the whole
engineering problem with washing a wall, since anything sprayed on it wants to
run straight off. The active component is chosen for what is growing and what it
is growing on: algae and mildew on vinyl take a different approach from lichen on
cedar or biological film on stucco.

The mildest chemistry that will actually do the job is the right one, and that is
a judgement made at the wall rather than a single mix used on every house. On
cedar, on some coated surfaces, and anywhere the runoff is going somewhere
sensitive, the gentler and slower option is the correct one even though it costs
more time.

What matters as much as the product is the process around it: pre-wetting,
covering, controlling where it goes, and rinsing thoroughly at the end. A
carefully applied ordinary solution does less harm than an aggressive one applied
carelessly.

## Sequence, if you are doing more than one thing

Siding comes third: roof, then gutters, then walls, then glass, then hard
surfaces last. Washing walls before the roof and gutters means washing them
twice, because everything above sheds downward. Booking together is cheaper than
separate visits because access is set up once.

## On the day

Before anything is wet, we walk the elevations with you and agree the scope,
check that windows and doors are closed, look for damaged screens, open vents,
compromised electrical covers and anything on the walls that needs to come off.
Furniture, planters and grills move away from the working elevation — we can do
that or you can, and it is worth saying which you would prefer when booking.

Planting gets its pre-wet. Covers go on where they are needed. Then the work
proceeds elevation by elevation rather than jumping around the house, which
sounds obvious and is the difference between an evenly cleaned building and one
with patches.

At the end everything is rinsed down, the ground is left as we found it, and if
you are there we walk it with you. If you are not, we send photographs. Either
way, if something is not right, we would much rather hear it while the equipment
is still on your driveway.

Most single-storey houses are a half day. Two-storey houses with detail work run
most of a day. It is not a job we like to rush, because the failure mode of
rushing a soft wash is streaking, and streaks on a wall are more visible than the
growth was.

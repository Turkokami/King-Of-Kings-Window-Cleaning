Moss is the defining roof problem of this part of Washington. Not algae, which is
mostly a staining issue, and not lichen, which is slow and stubborn but sits on
the surface. Moss is the one that takes roofs apart, and it does it by a
mechanism most homeowners have never had explained to them.

This page is about that mechanism, what can be done about it, and why the right
answer takes months rather than an afternoon.

## What moss actually is

Moss is a bryophyte — a primitive plant with no vascular system, no flowers and,
importantly, **no true roots**. What looks like a root system is a mat of
rhizoids, fine filaments whose job is anchorage rather than nutrient uptake.
Moss does not feed on your roof. It has no interest in the shingle at all.

What it needs is moisture, shade and something to hold onto. A north-facing
composition roof under conifer cover in a region that receives three feet of rain
a year supplies all three, permanently.

Two properties of moss matter for what it does to a building.

**It holds water.** Moss absorbs and retains many times its own dry weight, and
it releases that water slowly. A moss cushion on a roof is a sponge sitting
against the roofing material, keeping it wet long after the rain has stopped and
long after an unmossed roof would have dried.

**It grows outward and downward into gaps.** Moss colonises edges, seams and any
place where two surfaces meet, because those places hold moisture longest. On a
shingle roof, that means the lower edge of every course.

## The mechanism — why moss is different from algae

An asphalt shingle roof works on one simple principle: overlapping courses, each
lapping the one below, so water always runs downhill across the top of the
assembly and never gets underneath it. Everything else — the underlayment, the
flashing details — is backup for when that principle is compromised.

Moss compromises it directly.

Growth establishes along the exposed lower edge of a shingle, where debris
collects and moisture lingers. As the colony expands, it grows **back under the
leading edge of the course above**. Moss is soft, but it is persistent and it is
growing against a shingle that is only held down by an adhesive strip and its own
weight. Over seasons, the colony physically lifts the course.

Once a course is lifted, two things follow.

The first is that the seal is broken, and a lifted shingle is a shingle the wind
can get under.

The second is worse. The moss sitting in that gap is saturated, and moss wicks
water by capillary action — **upward, against gravity**, into the space under the
course. Water that gets under a shingle is water in contact with the
underlayment and eventually the deck. It does not usually announce itself as a
drip. It announces itself years later as rot in the sheathing.

Then there is **freezing**. Water held in moss at the eaves and in the valleys
expands when it freezes, and it expands in a gap that moss has already opened.
Across the service area this matters; in the foothills — Deming, Acme, Kendall,
Maple Falls, Glacier — it is a routine annual event.

None of this applies to algae. Algae stains a roof and holds a little surface
moisture. Moss dismantles it.

## Where moss grows on your roof, and why there

Moss is not evenly distributed and its pattern tells you something.

**North-facing slopes**, first and worst, because they get the least sun and stay
damp longest.

**Under and near overhanging trees**, where shade and constant organic debris
combine. The debris matters as much as the shade — it holds moisture and gives
moss a seedbed on an otherwise unfriendly surface.

**Valleys**, where two roof planes meet and water, needles and grit all
concentrate.

**Along the eaves**, the coldest part of the roof and the last part to dry.

**Around penetrations** — chimneys, vents, skylights, dormers — where flashing
creates edges and shelter.

**The lower third generally**, on any roof, because water travelling down the
slope has been in contact longest by the time it gets there.

If moss is confined to the north slope and the eaves, the roof is behaving
normally for this region. If it is across the whole roof including south-facing
planes, the roof has been wet for a long time and there is usually a shade or
debris problem driving it.

## How the moss actually comes off

Three stages, in order, and the order is the whole method.

**One — the bulk is lifted by hand.** Thick moss cushions carry real weight and
hold a great deal of water, and there is no reason to leave that sitting on a
roof for three months waiting for the weather. It comes off by hand, gently,
working with the direction of the courses rather than against them. Hand-lifting
loose bulk is not scraping: the mass releases with light pressure because moss
has no true roots holding it, and nothing is being dragged across the granule
surface. The same applies to the mats that build on walkways and pavers, where
the bulk lifts off in sheets.

**Two — what remains is treated in place.** Hand-lifting takes the cushion. It
does not take the rhizoid mat left behind on the surface, and it does not reach
the growth under the leading edge of the course — which, as described above, is
the part actually damaging the roof. That is what the treatment is for. It kills
the remaining growth including the part no tool should be going anywhere near.

**Three — the weather finishes it.** The killed remnant dries, shrinks, loses its
grip and sheds progressively under normal wind and rain over the following weeks
and months. Nothing scours the surface. Nothing lifts a course. The granules stay
where they belong.

What we do not do, at any stage, is force it:

**Pressure washing** blasts granules off the shingle and drives water up under
the courses — the exact gap the moss has been opening. It is the single worst
thing that can be done to an asphalt roof and it voids most manufacturer
warranties. There is more on the [roof cleaning](/services/roof-cleaning/) page.

**Scraping with tools** takes granules wherever moss is bonded, and reaching the
sub-course growth with a blade means lifting shingles that are meant to stay
sealed down. A scraped roof looks transformed on the day and has lost years.

**Wire brushing** does both at once and is the fastest way to age a roof.

The difference between lifting bulk moss by hand and scraping a roof is the
difference between taking the weight off and taking the surface off. It is worth
asking any contractor which of the two they are proposing.

The cost of this method is patience for the final stage, which is why we explain
the timeline before taking the booking rather than after.

## What the visit involves

- **Assessment first** — what is growing, how established it is, which roof
  material, what the access is, and whether the roof is in a condition to be
  treated at all
- **Protection** — planting pre-wetted, sensitive planting covered, runoff
  routed away from where it should not go
- **Treatment applied at low pressure**, chosen for the roofing material and the
  catchment
- **Dwell time** — the solution has to stay wet on the growth long enough to work
- **Gentle rinse** where the material and the method call for it
- **Loose surface debris cleared** from valleys and around penetrations, by hand
- **Gutters checked**, because treated moss will shed into them over the following
  weeks
- **A condition note** on anything visible — lifted or missing shingles, exposed
  fasteners, damaged flashing, deteriorated ridge

## The timeline, honestly

**Days one to seven.** The moss dies. Visually, very little changes. This is the
stage that worries people who were expecting an immediate transformation, and it
is normal.

**Weeks two to six.** The moss browns, dries and becomes brittle. It starts to
break up and shed in wind and rain. Debris in the gutters increases noticeably.

**Months two to three.** The bulk of the dead moss has weathered off. The roof
looks substantially different from how it looked on the day.

**Months six to twelve.** Whatever was most firmly anchored — and any lichen
present — finishes releasing.

Gutter clearing is normally scheduled *after* moss treatment for exactly this
reason. Doing it before means doing it twice.

## Keeping it off

Three levers, in descending order of how much difference they make.

**Cut back what overhangs the roof.** This is the big one and it is not a product.
Branches over a roof block drying sun, drop organic debris that gives moss a
seedbed, and keep humidity high at the surface. Trimming them back extends the
interval between treatments more than anything else available.

**Keep the roof clear of debris.** Needles and leaves accumulating in valleys and
along the eaves are the substrate moss establishes in. A roof that gets cleared
does not offer moss anywhere to start.

**Metal at the ridge.** Zinc or copper strips fitted at the ridge release trace
ions into runoff that moss struggles to establish in. Useful as prevention on a
clean roof, largely useless against established growth, and limited to the paths
the water actually takes. Worth fitting during a reroof. Fitted by roofers.

What does not work: moss killers applied once and expected to last, gutter guards
(unrelated problem), and the various sprays sold for lawns, which are formulated
for a different surface and can stain roofing.

## Moss, lichen and algae are not the same problem

Quickly, because they get conflated and the treatments differ:

**Moss** is the green cushion. Structural. Treated and weathered off.

**Lichen** is the flat, crusty, pale grey-green patch. It anchors into the
surface rather than sitting on it, is the most tenacious of the three, and
typically takes six to twelve months to release after treatment. More on
telling lichen from moss.

**Algae** is the black vertical streaking. A staining problem rather than a
structural one, and the fastest to respond. Covered on the
[roof cleaning](/services/roof-cleaning/) page.

Most roofs in this region carry at least two of the three, which is why the
assessment matters and why a single quoted price for "roof cleaning" without
anyone looking at the roof tells you very little.

## When to treat

**Early autumn** is the best window. Treating before the wet season means the
growth goes into the rainy months dead rather than feeding on them, and the
winter weather does the removal work for free.

**Spring** is the second-best, once the weather has settled enough for the
solution to dwell rather than being rinsed off by the next shower.

**Not during a freeze**, and not on a hot roof in full sun, where solution
evaporates before it can act.

**On a three-to-five-year cycle** for most roofs here — nearer three under heavy
conifer cover or at elevation, nearer five on an exposed roof on the flats.

## What it costs, and what moves it

Quoted on site, always, because moss loading is the single most variable thing we
price. The drivers:

1. **How much moss, and how established.** Light colonisation along one eave is a
   modest job. Cushions two or three inches deep across a north slope and packed
   into the valleys is a different one.
2. **Roof area, pitch and complexity.** Valleys, dormers, multiple planes and
   levels all add time — and valleys are exactly where the heaviest moss is.
3. **Roofing material**, per the section above.
4. **Access, and what is underneath.** Mature planting, water features, a
   neighbouring property at the drip line, or a steep drop below the eaves all
   mean more protection work.
5. **Catchment.** In the Lake Whatcom watershed the method is constrained and
   constrained methods take longer.
6. **Whether the gutters go with it.** They usually should, and doing both in one
   mobilisation is cheaper than two visits.

## Safety, and why we mostly stay off the roof

Our default is to treat from ladders and extension equipment rather than by
walking the surface, and that preference is about the roof as much as about the
operator. Foot traffic on asphalt shingle causes granule loss in its own right —
worse on a cold roof, where shingles are brittle, and worse on a hot one, where
they are soft. A moss-covered roof adds a slick layer over both.

Where a roof does have to be walked, that is a judgement made on the day, in the
conditions on the day, with the right footwear and anchor arrangement. If it is
not safe, we reschedule rather than improvise. That is not caution for its own
sake — it is that the alternative is somebody falling off a two-storey house.

## When a roof should not be treated

Sometimes the honest answer is not to do the job.

A roof at the end of its service life — widespread granule loss, curling or
cupping shingles, brittleness, multiple patched areas — can be damaged by any
treatment, and treating it does not extend it enough to justify the money. Heavy
moss on a roof in that condition is usually a symptom of age rather than the
cause of it.

We would rather tell you that at the quote and lose the job than take payment for
work that would be better spent on a roof. That conversation happens standing in
your driveway, before anything is booked.

We do not repair roofs, replace shingles, reseal flashings or carry out
inspections or condition reports. What we report is what someone who was on or at
the roof could see, offered as an observation.

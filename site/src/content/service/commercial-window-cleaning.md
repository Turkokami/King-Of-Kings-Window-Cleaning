Commercial glass is a different product from residential glass, sold to a
different person for a different reason. A homeowner books a window clean because
the windows are dirty. A business books one because the front of the building is
the first thing a customer sees, and because nobody on the payroll should be
standing on a ladder in the entrance.

What follows is how the work runs, what the frequency should be, and where the
honest limits are.

## Who this is for

**Retail and storefronts** — the largest part of this work. Downtown Bellingham,
Fairhaven, Barkley Village, the Guide Meridian corridor, and the main streets of
Ferndale, Lynden, Blaine, Mount Vernon, Burlington, Sedro-Woolley and Anacortes.
Entrance glass, display windows, and anything the customer touches on the way in.

**Restaurants, cafés and bars.** Higher-frequency work, driven by grease film,
condensation and heavy hand contact, and almost always scheduled outside service
hours.

**Offices and professional suites** — medical and dental practices, legal and
accounting offices, clinics, agencies. Interior and exterior on a monthly or
quarterly cycle, usually with more interior glass than a retail unit and more
constraints on when a stranger can be in the space.

**Multi-tenant and mixed-use buildings**, where the common glass belongs to the
landlord and the tenant glass belongs to the tenant, and the scope needs to be
written down clearly before the first visit.

**Apartment blocks, HOAs and condominium buildings** — common areas, lobbies,
stairwell glass and, where it is in scope, unit exteriors.

**Auto dealerships and showrooms**, which are almost all glass and where the
appearance of it is directly part of the sales floor.

**Schools, churches and institutional buildings**, often on a termly or seasonal
rather than monthly cycle.

**Vacation rentals and hospitality**, where turnover scheduling governs.

## Frequency, and why one interval for all the glass is wrong

The most common mistake in commercial window cleaning is treating a building's
glass as one surface with one schedule. It is not, and paying to clean all of it
at the fast-soiling interval is expensive.

**Entrance and door glass** is the fast-soiling surface. Hands, weather, foot
traffic, spray off the sidewalk, and in food service, grease carried on the air.
This is what customers stand directly in front of and it is where dirty glass
actually costs you something. Weekly or fortnightly for most retail; more for
food service.

**Ground-floor display and frontage glass** soils more slowly — weather and road
film rather than contact. Fortnightly to monthly suits most.

**Upper-storey exterior glass** soils slowly and is mostly weather-driven.
Quarterly is usually sufficient, twice a year on some buildings.

**Interior glass** depends entirely on the space. An office with partition glass
and a lot of hand contact needs it regularly; a warehouse mezzanine does not.

The sensible arrangement is a mixed schedule: entrance glass frequently, the rest
of the building on a longer cycle, all under one agreement with one invoice. That
is nearly always cheaper than the flat interval, and it puts the money where the
customer's eye actually lands.

## What a scheduled route actually gives you

The value of a commercial agreement is not a lower per-visit rate. It is
predictability, and there are four parts to it.

**The same crew.** A crew that has cleaned your building before knows which
entrance is unlocked at seven, which panel is out of square, where the water and
the outlet are, which planter has to move, and what the manager notices first.
That knowledge is worth real time on every visit, and it is why a rotating cast
of subcontractors never quite gets there.

**A schedule you can rely on.** The point of a route is that it happens without
anybody chasing it. If you are calling to ask when the window cleaner is coming,
the arrangement has already failed.

**Working around your hours, not through them.** Before opening for retail, after
close for hospitality, agreed quiet windows for offices and clinics.

**One invoice.** Monthly, against an agreed schedule and rate.

## Working outside business hours

Most of this work happens early. Storefront glass gets cleaned before the doors
open, so that the first customer of the day sees the result and nobody has to
step around equipment.

That means keys, codes, alarm arrangements and access agreements — all of which
get settled in writing before the first visit rather than improvised on the
morning. For premises where a crew cannot be unaccompanied, we schedule to a
window when someone is there.

It also means we are frequently the first people at a property in the morning,
which puts us in a position to tell you about things you would otherwise find out
later: a broken pane, a forced door, graffiti, water where it should not be,
a light out over the entrance. Not part of the service, but a reasonable thing to
pass on, and we do.

## Multi-storey work, and where we stop

Worth being direct about the limit, because it is a real one.

We clean multi-storey glass to the height a water-fed pole system reaches —
practically, that covers most of the commercial building stock in Whatcom and
Skagit County, which is predominantly two to four storeys. Pure water on a
telescopic pole means upper glass is cleaned from the ground: no ladders in a
pedestrian entrance, no lift blocking a parking area, no disruption below.

**We do not do high-rise work.** Rope access, bosun's chair, suspended platform
and swing-stage work are a separate discipline with their own certification and
equipment, and taking on a building that needs them would be irresponsible. If
your building is beyond pole height, we will tell you at the walk-round and you
should engage a high-rise specialist.

That limit almost never bites in this market. It occasionally does in downtown
Bellingham, and being straight about it is better than finding out on the day.

## Post-construction and new fit-out

A different job from maintenance cleaning, priced and approached separately.

New commercial glazing arrives carrying mortar and grout splash, silicone smears,
adhesive residue from factory labels, paint overspray and construction dust.
Getting it off is skilled work, and getting it wrong is expensive: modern
commercial glazing is heavily tempered, and tempered glass carries latent surface
debris from the tempering process that aggressive blade work can drag across the
pane, producing fine scratching over the whole surface. The scratches are caused
by the tool; the vulnerability came from the factory. The resulting argument
about who pays for a full storefront's glass is one nobody enjoys.

So post-construction is quoted as its own scope, by people who know what is under
the blade, and it is not folded into a first regular visit as though it were the
same thing.

## Beyond the glass

Most commercial buildings need more than window cleaning, and the same access
visit can cover it:

**Entrance walkways and hardstanding** — see
[pressure washing](/services/pressure-washing/). On a commercial property this is
a liability question as much as an appearance one: a mossy entrance step in
November is a slip claim waiting to happen, and "it is on the maintenance
schedule" is a much better position than the alternative.

**Building exteriors** — see [house washing](/services/house-washing/), which
covers commercial siding and cladding with the same soft-wash method.

**Gutters and roof drainage** — see
[gutter cleaning](/services/gutter-cleaning/). Commercial buildings frequently
have internal drains, parapet scuppers and flat-roof drainage rather than hung
gutters, which is a different inspection and a different clearing method.

Bundling these into one scheduled arrangement is generally cheaper and always
easier to administer than managing three contractors against three calendars.

## Why a business should care beyond appearance

Not a hard sell, but the reasons are real and they are not the ones usually
given.

**Glass is the first impression, and it is unconscious.** Nobody walks into a
shop thinking about the windows. They form an impression of how well the business
is run in the first few seconds, and the front of the building supplies most of
it. Clean glass does not get noticed; dirty glass does.

**Natural light is a working condition.** Filmed glass measurably reduces the
light entering a space, and in a region with this little winter daylight, that
matters more here than it would somewhere sunnier. Offices with clean glass are
simply better places to spend a February afternoon.

**Nobody on your payroll should be doing it.** This is the practical one. Asking
a member of staff to clean the outside of a shopfront means a person on a ladder
or a chair, usually untrained, usually without the right equipment, on your
premises and your liability. It is not a saving.

**Display and merchandising depend on it.** A window display seen through filmed
glass is a display you paid for and are not getting.

**Landlords and leases.** A commercial lease frequently makes the tenant
responsible for the condition of the frontage, and some make it explicit. Worth
knowing what yours says.

## What changes the price

1. **Pane count and total glass area.**
2. **Frequency.** A weekly route has a lower per-visit rate than an occasional
   call-out, because the glass is being maintained rather than restored.
3. **Interior, exterior, or both.**
4. **Height**, and how much is reachable from the ground.
5. **Access constraints** — out-of-hours work, escorted access, security
   procedures, restricted parking, pedestrian management.
6. **Frontage type.** A dealership showroom and a small office suite with the
   same square footage are not the same job.
7. **Condition at the start.** The first visit on a neglected building is a
   restoration visit and is priced accordingly, once.

## How the pure-water system works on a commercial building

Worth explaining, because it is the reason upper-storey commercial glass can be
done without disrupting the ground floor.

Water is filtered to remove all its dissolved minerals, then fed up a telescopic
carbon-fibre pole to a brush at the top. The brush agitates the glass, the pure
water carries the dirt off, and because there is nothing dissolved in the water,
it dries leaving nothing at all — no spotting, no residue, no squeegee needed.
The glass is left to sheet dry.

For a commercial building that means several practical things. Nobody is on a
ladder in a doorway. There is no lift occupying a parking bay or a sidewalk. The
work happens from the ground, quickly, and the operator is watching the whole
frontage rather than one pane at a time. Frames and seals get rinsed as part of
it, which conventional squeegee work does not do.

The trade-off is that pure water needs a slight dwell — a pane cleaned this way
looks wet and unfinished for a few minutes before it dries clear. On a shopfront
being cleaned before opening this is a non-issue. It occasionally surprises a
manager watching from inside.

Ground-floor and entrance glass is generally still done conventionally, with
detergent and a squeegee, because hand contact and grease respond better to
physical contact and because the result is instant on the glass people are about
to walk past.

## Scope, written down

The single most common source of friction in commercial cleaning agreements is
scope that was agreed verbally and remembered differently. Ours gets written
down, and it names:

- **Which glass** — exterior, interior, or both, and to what height
- **Which elevations**, including whether rear and service frontages are in
- **Whose glass**, in a multi-tenant building — landlord common areas, tenant
  frontages, or both
- **Frequency by area**, since it is usually mixed
- **Access arrangements** — times, keys, codes, escort requirements
- **What is excluded** — signage, awnings, skylights, atrium glazing, anything
  above pole height
- **Rate and invoicing cycle**

It takes twenty minutes at the walk-round and it prevents every argument that
would otherwise happen in month four, when somebody assumed the rear elevation
and the stairwell glass were included and nobody had written down that they were
not.

## What we do not do

- We do not do high-rise work requiring rope access, suspended platforms or
  swing stages.
- We do not clean signage faces, awnings or canopies as part of standard window
  cleaning — they are quoted separately where we can do them.
- We do not repair glazing, replace failed sealed units, or fix door hardware. We
  report what we see.
- We do not carry out building inspections or condition reports.
- We do not fold post-construction cleaning into a maintenance visit.

## Getting started

A walk-round first, on site, with whoever will be managing the arrangement. We
count the glass, look at the access, identify what is in scope and what is not,
and talk about frequency by area rather than proposing one interval for the whole
building.

You get a written scope, a schedule and a rate. If insurance documentation is
needed for a landlord or a property manager, that is provided up front. Then the
route runs, and the intention is that you stop thinking about it.

There is no minimum term and no cancellation clause to argue about. A scheduled
arrangement should hold because it is working, not because it is difficult to
leave.

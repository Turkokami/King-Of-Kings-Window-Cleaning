Metal roofing has taken a large share of new construction in Whatcom and Skagit
County, and it turns up on everything from architect-designed homes on the water
to barns, shops and outbuildings across the valleys. It is chosen for longevity,
and it delivers on that — a properly installed metal roof can outlast two or
three asphalt roofs.

That longevity depends almost entirely on one thing: the factory coating. Which
means the entire question of how to clean a metal roof reduces to how to remove
what is on it without harming what is under it.

## The coating is the roof

A metal roof panel is not really a metal roof. It is a thin steel or aluminium
substrate carrying a multi-layer factory finish, and that finish is doing all the
work that matters.

On steel panels there is usually a metallic coating first — galvanised zinc, or a
zinc-aluminium alloy — bonded to the steel to stop it corroding. Over that goes a
primer, and then the visible paint system. On premium roofs that top layer is
typically a Kynar-type fluoropolymer, which is what carries the long colour and
chalk warranties; on more economical panels it is a polyester system, which
performs well but weathers faster.

Every one of those layers is applied under factory conditions and cannot be
recreated on a roof. The steel underneath is entirely ordinary steel: expose it
and it does what ordinary steel does in a climate with this much rain.

So the working rule for cleaning is simple. **Anything that dulls, scratches,
strips or chemically etches that finish has taken life off the roof**, regardless
of how clean the panel looks afterwards.

This is also why aggressive cleaning of metal is more tempting and more damaging
than aggressive cleaning of shingle. Metal feels robust underfoot and under a
jet. It does not flake or shed granules to warn you. The damage is to a coating
measured in fractions of a millimetre, and it shows up years later as chalking,
fading and edge corrosion.

## What actually grows and collects on metal

The panel faces stay comparatively clean, and that is genuine — a smooth, coated,
well-pitched metal surface sheds water fast and gives moss very little to hold
onto. The work is at the details.

**The seams.** On standing seam roofs the raised ribs create channels that trap
needles, leaves and grit. Organic material sits in them, holds moisture, and moss
and lichen establish along the seam line. This is the classic metal roof
appearance problem: clean panels with green or black lines between them.

**The fasteners.** On exposed-fastener corrugated and ribbed roofs — very common
on agricultural and outbuilding roofs here — every screw head is a small ledge
with a rubber washer under it. Debris collects at each one, and each one is a
place where growth establishes and where water can eventually get in as the
washer ages.

**The flashings and transitions.** Valleys, ridges, hips, sidewall and headwall
flashings, chimney and vent penetrations, and anywhere a roof plane changes
angle. These collect everything that moves down the roof, and they are where a
metal roof is most likely to have a real problem hiding under the debris.

**Low-slope sections.** Porch roofs, dormer tops, and shallow additions where
water does not run off briskly. Debris sits, and so does moisture.

**Streaking.** Dark lines running down from seams, fasteners and edges. A mix of
organic residue and oxidised material carried in runoff, bonded to the coating.
Plain water does nothing to it.

**Tree sap and needle staining**, on roofs under conifers — tannin marking that
sets into a tea-coloured stain.

**Salt film**, at the marine edge in Blaine, Birch Bay, Semiahmoo, Anacortes and
on the islands. Metal roofs near salt water need more attention than metal roofs
inland, and cut edges and fasteners are the vulnerable points.

**Chalking.** Not growth. The gradual UV breakdown of the paint system's surface
into a fine powder. It rubs off on a hand and it is the coating aging. Washing
removes the loose chalk and improves appearance; it does not restore the finish,
and a heavily chalked roof is telling you something about where it is in its life.

## The method

Soft washing, and on metal the emphasis falls even harder on the "soft" than it
does on shingle.

Low pressure — roughly garden-hose level. A mild cleaning solution chosen for the
coating type rather than for speed, applied and given time to dwell so that the
chemistry does the work. Gentle agitation only where it is needed, with soft
bristle, never abrasive pads, wire brushes or anything that will mark a painted
surface. Then a thorough rinse.

Two details specific to metal:

**Direction matters.** Metal panels lap, and the laps shed water downward. Work
proceeds down the slope, and rinsing is directed down-slope, so that nothing is
being pushed up under a lap or behind a flashing. A pressure washer aimed
uphill at a metal roof is putting water exactly where the roof is designed to
keep it out.

**Dissimilar metals matter.** Copper and steel do not get along: copper runoff
onto a galvanised or steel panel causes galvanic corrosion. If there is copper
anywhere above the panels — flashing, a vent, a decorative element — that is
worth knowing before choosing a cleaning approach, and worth knowing generally.

## Why we mostly stay off it

Metal roofs are more hazardous to walk than they look and easier to damage.

They are **slippery**, disproportionately so when wet, frosted, or carrying any
organic film — which is precisely the condition of a roof that needs cleaning.
The smooth coated surface offers very little grip and a metal roof does not
forgive a slide the way a rough shingle does.

They also **dent and oil-can**. Standing between supports on a panel spanning
purlins can leave a permanent deformation, and on a standing seam roof, weight in
the wrong place can distort a seam and compromise the way it sheds water.

So the default is ladders, poles and extension equipment. Where the roof does
have to be walked, it is walked over structure rather than mid-span, in the right
footwear, in the right conditions, and by somebody who has looked at the panel
profile first.

## What we look at while we are there

Not an inspection — an observation from somebody at the roof, reported with
photographs:

- **Backed-out or missing fasteners**, and aged or split washers on exposed-
  fastener roofs. These are the most common source of leaks on that roof type and
  they are visible.
- **Surface rust** at cut edges, panel ends, scratches and fastener penetrations.
- **Sealant failure** at flashings, penetrations and end laps.
- **Panel movement** — metal expands and contracts considerably with temperature,
  and fixings can work loose over years of it.
- **Debris packed into valleys and behind flashings**, which is cleared as part of
  the work.
- **Coating condition** — chalking, fading, or areas weathering differently.

We do not repair any of it. We do not replace fasteners, apply sealant, treat
rust, or touch up coating. Those are roofing jobs, and the value of the visit is
that somebody looked and told you.

## When to book

**Spring or early autumn**, on a mild overcast day. Metal gets extremely hot in
direct sun, which flash-dries solution before it can work and makes the surface
unpleasant and more hazardous to work on. It also gets slick with frost early on
cold mornings.

**Every three to five years** for most metal roofs here — longer on an exposed
roof away from trees, shorter under conifers, at the marine edge, or where there
is a low-slope section collecting debris.

**Before the wet season**, if there is heavy organic material in the seams, so it
is not sitting wet all winter.

**After a nearby construction project** or a windstorm.

## Metal roofs in this climate specifically

A few things about Whatcom and Skagit that shape how metal behaves here.

**High rainfall is good for metal.** A smooth coated surface at a decent pitch
sheds water fast, and the frequent rinsing this region provides genuinely keeps
the panel faces clean. Metal is a sensible choice here and this is a large part
of why it has taken so much of the new-build market.

**Conifer debris is the offsetting problem.** Needles do not slide off a roof the
way rain does — they lodge in seams, behind flashings and against any raised
detail, and then they hold moisture in exactly the places the roof would
otherwise stay dry.

**Thermal cycling is moderate but constant.** Metal expands and contracts with
temperature, and the mild but variable weather here means a lot of small
movements rather than a few large ones. Over decades that works fixings loose and
fatigues sealant.

**Freeze events at elevation.** In the foothills — Deming, Acme, Kendall, Maple
Falls, Glacier — water held in debris-packed seams and valleys freezes, and ice
in a seam is a problem a clean seam does not have. Metal also sheds snow in
sudden slides rather than gradually, which is worth knowing about what sits below
the eaves.

**Salt at the marine edge.** Blaine, Birch Bay, Semiahmoo, Anacortes, Guemes and
Samish Island. Salt aerosol is corrosive, it concentrates at cut edges and
fastener penetrations where the coating is thinnest or breached, and it stays
damp. Coastal metal roofs benefit from more frequent cleaning than inland ones,
and rinsing salt off is doing real preservation work rather than cosmetic work.

**Agricultural exposure** through the valleys — dust, ammonia in livestock
buildings, and chemical drift — is harder on coatings than residential exposure
is.

## What changes the price

1. **Roof area and the number of planes.**
2. **Panel type** — standing seam, exposed-fastener corrugated, ribbed, or
   architectural profile. Seam count and fastener count both drive time.
3. **Pitch and access**, and how much can be reached without going onto the roof.
4. **Condition.** Light film is quick; established growth in every seam is not.
5. **Detail count** — flashings, valleys, penetrations, skylights, dormers.
6. **Coating type**, since a more delicate finish means a gentler and slower
   method.

## Why metal gets pressure washed more than it should

Worth naming the pattern directly, because it is the single most common way these
roofs get damaged.

Shingle roofs deter people. They look fragile, they shed granules visibly when
mistreated, and most contractors have absorbed the message that pressure washing
them is wrong. Metal has none of those warning signs. It is hard, it rings under
a boot, it does not flake, and a jet of water bounces off it looking like it is
doing a great job.

So metal is where the pressure washer comes out. It cleans fast, the result on
the day is impressive, and nothing appears to have gone wrong.

What has gone wrong is invisible for years. The finish has been micro-abraded and
partially stripped, so it chalks earlier and fades unevenly. Water has been
driven up under panel laps and behind flashings, into an assembly detailed for
downward flow. On an exposed-fastener roof, aged washers have been disturbed. And
at cut edges and scratches where the substrate was already marginally exposed,
the protective layer has been thinned further.

The result arrives as premature corrosion and a roof reaching maybe two thirds of
the life it was sold with. By then nobody connects it to a cleaning done eight
years earlier.

If you are getting quotes for a metal roof, the useful question is not what it
costs. It is what pressure they intend to use and what they are putting on the
coating. A contractor who answers that specifically has thought about the roof; a
contractor who has not is going to treat it like a driveway.

## On the day

We look at the roof before quoting rather than pricing from a square-foot figure,
because panel profile, coating type and detail count all change the job and none
of them are visible from a phone call.

On the day: access is set up, the ground below is protected, planting is pre-wet
and covered where needed, and downspout discharge points are considered so that
runoff goes somewhere sensible. Then the work runs plane by plane, top to bottom,
with the solution given real dwell time rather than being rushed off.

Metal rinses more completely than shingle does, which is one of the pleasant
things about the job — there is no granular surface holding residue, so a
thorough rinse genuinely leaves a clean panel. Seams, valleys and flashings get
cleared by hand of whatever the wash loosens.

At the end we walk the property with you if you are there, and send photographs
of anything worth seeing if you are not — particularly fastener condition, edge
rust and sealant, since those are the things you would want to know about and
cannot see from the ground.

A typical residential metal roof is a half day to a day. Agricultural buildings
depend entirely on span and fastener count, and a large barn roof is realistically
a full day or more of careful work rather than a quick pass.


## What each profile needs

| Profile | Where attention goes | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Standing seam | Seam bases, flashings, low-slope transitions | Panel face is nearly self-clearing; the debris beside it is the job |
| Exposed-fastener corrugated or ribbed | Several hundred washers, plus the panel | Washer condition is the maintenance item and the reason to look |
| Metal shingle and tile profiles | Surface relief that holds debris | Slower clean, more places to check |
| Stone-coated steel | Treated like asphalt shingle, not like metal | Granules dislodge under pressure |
| Copper and zinc | Conservative or declined | The patina is the finish; uneven removal is worse than the soiling |

Cleaning a metal roof is preservation as much as appearance — it is the organic
matter and salt in the details that turn into corrosion.

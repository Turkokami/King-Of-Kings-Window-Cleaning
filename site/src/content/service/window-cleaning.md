Clean glass is the cheapest thing you can do to a house that changes how it feels
inside. It is also the job most often done badly, because the difference between
a window that is clean and a window that only looks clean from four feet away is
mostly invisible until the sun hits it at the wrong angle.

We clean windows across Whatcom and Skagit County — Bellingham, Ferndale, Lynden
and Blaine through to Mount Vernon, Burlington, Sedro-Woolley and Anacortes — on
houses, rentals, storefronts and new builds. What follows is what the work
actually involves, what changes the price, and what we will and will not promise.

## What is on your glass, and why it matters which

Most window-cleaning frustration comes from treating three different problems as
one. They are not, and they do not come off the same way.

**Dirt** is anything sitting on the surface — dust, pollen, splash, cobweb, bird
mess, road grime. It has not bonded to anything. It comes off with water,
detergent and a squeegee, and every clean handles it.

**Film** is dirt that has combined with moisture and dried in place. This is the
one people underestimate. Pollen that got rained on and dried in the sun, road
film on a house near a highway, industrial haze near a refinery corridor,
agricultural dust after a dry harvest week — all of it ends up as film. Film does
not rinse. A pressure rinse moves it around and leaves a smear pattern, which is
why so many people report that their windows look *worse* after they have washed
them. Breaking film takes a detergent stage and physical contact.

**Deposit** is mineral, and it is a different category altogether. Sprinkler
overspray that dries on the glass, rain running off a mineral-stained sill, hard
water from a well — each leaves calcium and magnesium behind when the water
evaporates. Fresh deposit comes off with a treatment made for it. Old deposit
that has been through enough cycles begins to attack the glass surface, and at
that point it has stopped being a cleaning problem. **Etched glass cannot be
cleaned clear.** It looks cloudy in the same places no matter what you do, and
the honest answer is that it is a replacement decision.

We identify which of the three you have standing at the window, on the quote,
before any money changes hands.

## What is included in a standard clean

- **Exterior and interior glass** on every accessible opening
- **Screens removed, washed, dried and replaced** in the opening they came from
- **Tracks and sills vacuumed and wiped** — the grit that collects here is what
  grinds out weatherstripping and wears the rollers
- **Frames wiped** where they are washable
- **Cobweb removal** around the openings and the eaves directly above them
- **A walk-round with you at the end**, in daylight, at the window

Quoted separately because access and risk vary so much: skylights, glass
balustrades, conservatory and sunroom glass, storm windows, and
post-construction glass.

Not included, so nobody is surprised: **condensation between the panes of a
sealed unit.** That is a failed seal. The moisture is inside the glass unit, no
external cleaning reaches it, and anyone who tells you they can clean it is
selling you something that cannot work. It is a glazing repair.

## How the work is done

Two methods, chosen per window rather than per house, because the right tool for
a ground-floor sash is the wrong tool for a gable window over a deck.

### Traditional, for reachable glass

A detergent solution, a scrubber worked over the whole pane to break the film,
then a squeegee pulled in a controlled pattern to take the water and everything
in it off the glass in one movement, with the edges detailed by hand. There is
no substitute for physical contact when the problem is bonded film, and on
accessible glass this remains the fastest route to a genuinely clear pane.

### Water-fed pole, for height and obstruction

Upper storeys, and anything above a deck, a slope, a conservatory roof or a
planting bed, get a brush on a telescopic pole fed with purified water.

The purified water is the point, not the pole. Ordinary water carries dissolved
minerals; when it evaporates off the glass, the minerals stay behind as spots.
Strip them out and the water dries clean, which means the glass can be left to
sheet dry with no squeegee contact at all. It is what makes it possible to clean
a second-storey window properly without putting a ladder somewhere a ladder
should not go.

We carry our own water and filtration. On a property with hard water that matters
twice over — it means we are not putting the cause of the spotting back onto the
glass we just cleaned, and it means we can work on a house with the water shut
off or between tenants.

Where hard-water deposit is present and treatable, it is a separate stage before
the general clean, using a product made for mineral rather than for dirt.

## What changes the price

Quoted on site, every time, and it comes down to five things.

1. **Opening count.** The base, but only the base.
2. **Pane size.** A newer build with six large picture windows can take longer
   than an older house with thirty small ones. More glass, worse reach, more
   care needed on each pull.
3. **Access.** Ground-floor glass on open lawn is quick. Second-storey glass over
   a deck, a hedge or a slope is not, and a stairwell window is its own project.
4. **Condition on arrival.** Maintenance versus restoration. This is the single
   biggest swing, and it is why the first clean on a neglected property costs
   more than every clean after it.
5. **Extras.** Screens, tracks, skylights, balustrade glass, storm windows,
   french doors.

Two things worth knowing before you compare quotes. The second clean almost
always costs less than the first, because we are maintaining rather than
restoring. And a number quoted over the phone by someone who has not seen the
house is a guess — in an area where condition varies this much street to street,
usually a guess that favours whoever gave it.

## When to book

**Spring.** The most-booked window of the year and the most useful one. Winter in
this region leaves rain splash, organic film in the frame corners, and interior
condensation residue. Clearing it in March or April means the light half of the
year is spent looking through clean glass rather than at it.

**Early autumn.** After the summer dust has settled and before the weather turns.
In the agricultural parts of the county this is the more important of the two
visits, because harvest dust undoes a summer clean within weeks.

**Before listing a house.** Clean glass changes how a room photographs more than
almost anything else at the same price, because it changes the light in the room
rather than the contents of it.

**After construction or a remodel.** New builds leave the worst glass we see:
mortar splash, silicone, adhesive from factory labels, paint overspray, and
sometimes all four on brand-new panes still under warranty. It is a specialist
clean and it is quoted separately — see below for why doing it wrong is
expensive.

The one time we will tell you to wait is a hard frost. Cleaning glass at or below
freezing is bad for the glass and bad for the result.

## What separates a good clean from a fast one

A few things, none of them exotic, all of them the difference between glass that
looks clean for months and glass that looks clean until it rains.

**Detailing the edges.** Most of what people notice on a badly cleaned window is
in the last inch: a line of standing water at the bottom of the pane that dries
into a visible band, or a corner the squeegee never reached. That perimeter work
is slow and it is what the extra time buys.

**Working the whole pane, not the middle.** Film builds unevenly and is heaviest
at the bottom and the windward edge. A scrubber run over the centre and a fast
squeegee pull leaves a pane that reads clean from indoors and filthy from
outside.

**Handling the screens properly.** Screens hold an extraordinary amount of fine
dust and are the single most common reason someone believes their newly cleaned
windows are not clean. They also need to go back into the opening they came out
of — screens are made to the opening, not to a standard, and a swapped pair
rattles for the next decade.

**Knowing when to stop.** Etched glass, failed seals, latent scratching in
tempered panes, coatings that look like film — all of these are cases where more
effort makes things worse rather than better. Recognising them is worth more than
technique.

**Not putting hard water back on.** Rinsing with unpurified water on a hard-water
property re-creates the problem in the act of solving it.

## What we do not do

Stated plainly, because scope creep in this trade usually happens on the day and
under pressure:

- We do not clean between the panes of a sealed unit. That is a failed seal and a
  glazing repair.
- We do not remove paint, silicone or adhesive as part of a standard clean —
  that is post-construction work, priced and approached differently.
- We do not repair or reglaze windows, replace sashes, or fix failed hardware.
- We do not clean glass we cannot reach safely, and we identify those on the
  quote rather than on the day.
- We do not carry out inspections or issue condition reports. We will tell you
  what we can see, and that is an observation, not an assessment.

## What happens on the day

We arrive in a marked vehicle and walk the property with you before anything gets
wet, agreeing what is in scope and flagging anything we can see that will not
come off. Interior work means access to the rooms, and boots come off. Screens
come out and go back into the openings they came from — not into whichever
opening is nearest, which is how screens end up not fitting.

At the end we walk it with you, in daylight, at the windows. If something is not
right, that is the moment to say so, and we will fix it before we leave.

A Bellingham roof lives under someone's trees. Four acres in ten across the city
are canopy, and a lot of that canopy is conifer that never drops its shade in
winter, which is exactly when the rain arrives. Put those two things over the same
shingles and you get a roof that stays damp from October to spring and grows
something on nearly every plane.

This page covers what that growth is, how we take it off without taking the roof
with it, and the city rules that decide where the water goes afterwards.

## What grows on roofs here, and why so much of it?

Shade plus a long wet season, and Bellingham has both in quantity. The City puts
annual rainfall at about 36 inches, with roughly 80% of it falling between October
and March. That rain comes during the short, dim days, so a roof plane under trees
can go months without drying out fully.

The canopy is the other half. The City's urban forest figures put tree cover at
40% of the city, some 7,252 acres, and the native mix is heavy with species that
matter to a roof:

- **Western redcedar and Douglas-fir**, which shade year round and drop needles
  that collect in valleys and behind chimneys
- **Big-leaf maple and vine maple**, whose leaves mat down in autumn and hold water
  against the shingle
- **Red alder and black cottonwood**, fast growers that often end up overhanging a
  roof sooner than an owner expected
- **Sitka spruce**, one more evergreen on the list, and one more source of shade
  that does not lift in winter

That mix produces three kinds of growth. Black algae streaks run down from the
ridge. Lichen sits in flat, crusty patches. Moss builds into cushions along the
shaded eaves and in the valleys, and that is the one that does real harm. The
service hub for [roof cleaning](/services/roof-cleaning/) explains the biology of
each at length.

## Is the shade getting better or worse?

Worse, from a roof's point of view, and on purpose. The City's draft urban forest
plan sets a canopy target of 45% by 2050, up from the current 40%. That is good
news for the city and a fair warning for anyone with shingles under a young tree:
the shade line over your roof is more likely to grow in the next decade than to
shrink.

It is also why we tell people not to plan their roof around how it looked when
they bought the house. A lot that was open ten years ago may have a maple or an
alder reaching over the ridge now, and a roof that used to go five years between
treatments starts needing one every three.

## Which roofs in the city need the most care?

Material decides the method before the moss does.

**Composition shingle** is the bulk of what we treat. Its mineral granules are the
shingle's weather surface, and losing them shortens the roof. So everything we do
on asphalt is designed around keeping granules where they are, which rules out
pressure and rules out scraping. The
[composition asphalt shingle](/surface-library/composition-asphalt-shingle/)
profile goes into how that surface ages.

**Cedar shake and shingle** concentrates in the wooded south end, around Edgemoor,
Fairhaven, South Hill and out along Chuckanut Drive. Wood absorbs whatever is put on
it, and moss roots into the checks and the joints between courses. Cedar gets a
milder solution, careful control of how wet the wood gets, and no prying at the
growth. The [cedar shake roof](/surface-library/cedar-shake-roof/) profile has the
longer version.

**Metal**, where we find it, does not host algae the way asphalt does, but needles pile in the seams and the finish is sensitive to
the wrong chemistry.

Older districts deserve a note too. Bellingham has eight National Register historic
districts, including Fairhaven, Sehome Hill, South Hill, Eldridge and York. The
Cissna Cottages date from 1900 to 1904, and York was substantially built out by the
Great Depression. Older roofs are worth a slower look
at the quote before anything goes on them.

## What does the job include?

A standard visit on a Bellingham roof covers:

1. A walk around the property first, looking at the roof from every side and
   noting where planting, paths and drains sit below the drip line
2. Covering or berming any storm inlet in the work zone, and pre-wetting beds
   underneath
3. Clearing loose needles and leaf mats from valleys and from behind chimneys and
   skylights, by hand or blower
4. Low-pressure application of a solution chosen for the roofing material and the
   growth on it, left to dwell
5. A gentle rinse where the method calls for one, directed away from drains
6. Rinsing down the planting and hard surfaces below
7. A look at the result with you, and a straight account of what will keep
   changing over the next few months

Gutters, skylights and glass are separate lines on the quote but usually booked in
the same visit, because the roof sends material into all of them.

## How is a roof soft washed without damage?

With chemistry and patience instead of force.

The solution goes on at roughly garden-hose pressure. It kills the growth rather
than blasting it off, and then the Bellingham weather does the removal. Algae
lightens fast, often on the day. Moss browns over the following weeks and lets go
as the rain works on it, which is why the roof keeps looking better for a month or
two after we leave.

We keep foot traffic off the roof wherever the application can be done from ladders
and poles. Shingles are brittle when cold and soft when hot, and every step costs
granules either way. When a roof does have to be walked, the decision is made on
the day, in the conditions on the day. A saturated, mossy north slope in January is
one of the slickest surfaces in residential work, and we would rather come back
than gamble on it.

Moss that has worked its way under the courses is its own problem, and lifting
shingle tabs to reach it can crack them. If that is what we find, the
[moss under shingles](/services/moss-removal/moss-under-shingles/) page explains
what can realistically be done.

## What drives the price on a Bellingham roof?

We quote on site, because the factors vary street to street:

- **Size and shape.** A simple gable is quick; dormers, valleys, skylights and
  several levels add time.
- **Pitch.** A steep roof changes how the solution is delivered and how the crew is
  secured.
- **Material.** Asphalt, cedar and metal each take a different solution and a
  different pace.
- **Growth.** Light streaking is a short job. Thick moss in the valleys and old
  lichen take longer and sometimes a second pass.
- **What sits below.** Dense beds, a pond, a greenhouse or a neighbour's fence at
  the drip line all mean more protection work.
- **Watershed location.** Inside the Lake Whatcom watershed the plan is tighter and
  slower.

There is no figure on this page because a number without seeing the roof is a
guess. A roof treated on a regular cycle costs less each round than one being
rescued from ten years of growth.

## When is the right time of year?

Two windows work well here, and one is overlooked.

**Late spring** gives the solution dry spells long enough to dwell, and gets the
roof clean before summer.

**Early autumn** is the one people skip. Treating in September or early October
means the growth is dead before the wet half of the year starts, rather than
thriving on it. With four-fifths of the year's rain still to come, that timing buys
the most.

**Midwinter** is the time to look, not to treat. Moss is green, swollen and easy to
spot in December. In August it goes grey and flat and looks far less serious than it
is, which is how a lot of roofs get left a year too long.

We avoid treating in a freeze and on hot, sun-baked shingles, where the solution
dries before it has done anything.

## Where does the wash water go in Bellingham?

Not down the storm drain. Bellingham Municipal Code 15.42 prohibits pressure wash
water entering the storm system; it has to go to sanitary sewer or to a vegetated
area where the soil can filter it. A storm grate is not a sewer connection, and the
creeks that cross the city, Whatcom, Squalicum and Padden among them, are what a
careless rinse ends up threatening.

So inlets near the work get covered before we start, and rinse is steered onto lawn
and beds. The City also flags that buildings from 1950 to 1979 may contain PCBs,
and wash water from those can need licensed hazardous disposal, which is worth
raising at the quote on a house from that era.

On the east side the rules go further. The Lake Whatcom watershed is the city's
drinking water source, and Basin One carries a zero-phosphorus rule on fertilisers
and soil amendments. We plan watershed roofs with that in mind and keep the
products we choose there under closer scrutiny. The detail sits on our
[wash water and storm drains](/compliance/wash-water-and-storm-drains/) page, and
the neighbouring [Sudden Valley roof page](/locations/sudden-valley/roof-cleaning/)
covers the lakeside side of that catchment.

## What else tends to come up with a mossy roof?

Rarely just the roof.

**Gutters** take everything the treatment kills. Dead moss rides the rain into the
troughs for weeks, so we often clear them on the day and suggest a check after the
shedding tapers off. Our [Bellingham gutter page](/locations/bellingham/gutter-cleaning/)
covers the needle load separately.

**North walls** under the same trees usually carry green film too, because they
share the shade and the damp.

**Solar arrays** above a mossy roof collect needles and growth around the frames, and
the roof beneath them is often the hardest part to reach.

We will also tell you if a roof is too far gone to be worth cleaning. Widespread
granule loss, curling tabs or brittle shingles can be harmed by any method, and at
that point the useful call is to a roofer rather than to us.

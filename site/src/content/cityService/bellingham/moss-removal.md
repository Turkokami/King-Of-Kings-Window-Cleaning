Moss on a Bellingham roof is less a question of whether than of how much. The city
grows trees the way other places grow lawns, and it takes its rain in one long, cool,
grey stretch that suits moss perfectly. Leave a north-facing slope under a Douglas-fir
alone for a few winters and the green cushions along the shingle edges will be thick
enough to see from across the street.

The fix itself is straightforward, but it runs slower than most people expect, and the
fast versions do lasting harm. What follows is how we handle it in this city in
particular.

## Why does moss take hold so readily in Bellingham?

Because what moss needs is the normal condition of a Bellingham roof for half the
year. The city averages around 36 inches of rain annually, and roughly 80% of it falls
between October and March, not in cloudbursts that sluice off and clear, but as a long
run of damp days with little drying between them. Moss has no true roots and takes
nothing from the shingle. All it asks for is water held against a surface and enough
shade to stop it drying out.

The shade is where this city stands apart. The City counts about 40% of its area
under tree canopy, some 7,252 acres, and its urban forest plan aims to push that to
45% by 2050. Whatever you think of that as a resident, for a roof it means the
pressure is not going to ease off. Bellingham intends to get leafier, and roofs under
that canopy will keep needing attention.

## Which trees make it worse?

The conifers above all, and not only for their shade. The city's forest species are
western redcedar, Douglas-fir, big-leaf maple, vine maple, red alder, black cottonwood
and Sitka spruce. Fir and cedar drop needles and fine litter all year round, and that
debris packs into valleys, along the eaves and behind chimneys, where it soaks up water
and gives moss a seedbed on a surface that would otherwise be hard to colonise.

The broadleaf trees do their damage in one burst. Maples and alders let go of a
season's leaves over a few weeks each autumn, and a wet mat of leaves lying on the
shingles through November is a moss nursery. Street plantings here are mostly maples,
cherries and oaks, so even a house with no big conifer in the yard often has a street
tree reaching over the front slope.

A roof with nothing hanging over it goes far longer between treatments than one sitting
under branches. Tree work is not our trade, but trimming back what overhangs the roof
is the most useful single thing a homeowner can do to lengthen the interval.

## Where does moss start on a roof?

Wherever the roof stays wet longest:

- The north-facing slope, which gets the least direct sun all winter
- Along the eaves, the coldest part of the roof and the last to dry
- In the valleys, where water, needles and grit all collect
- Around chimneys, skylights and vents, where flashing forms ledges and shelter
- Beneath any branch that keeps dripping after the rain has stopped

Once it is established along a shingle edge, moss grows back beneath the course above
and starts to lift it. That hidden growth under the laps is the part that actually
damages a roof, and it is explained in detail on
[moss under shingles](/services/moss-removal/moss-under-shingles/).

## How does the moss come off without harming the roof?

In three stages, and none of them uses a pressure washer.

First, the heavy cushions are lifted away by hand, working down the slope with the
courses rather than against them. Thick moss holds a great deal of water and weight,
and there is no reason to leave it on the roof. Lifting that bulk is not the same as
scraping: moss is not rooted into the shingle, so it comes away under light pressure
and the granules stay where they are.

Second, what is left is treated at low pressure with a solution chosen for the roofing
material. That reaches the thin mat still clinging to the surface and the growth tucked
under the course edges, which no tool should be forced into.

Third, the weather finishes it. Over the following weeks and months the treated moss
browns, dries, loses its grip and washes off. On a Bellingham roof, for once, the
autumn rain is on your side.

The full method, and what we refuse to do (scraping, wire brushing, high pressure), is
on the main [moss removal](/services/moss-removal/) page. How asphalt roofs age and
shed granules is covered under
[composition asphalt shingle](/surface-library/composition-asphalt-shingle/).

## What about cedar roofs in the south end?

They need the gentlest handling of anything we work on. Cedar roofing and siding are
concentrated in the wooded south of the city, in Edgemoor, Fairhaven, South Hill and
out along Chuckanut Drive, and many of those roofs sit right under the fir and cedar
canopy that feeds moss in the first place.

Shakes and cedar shingles cannot take force. High pressure shreds the soft surface
fibres, and scraping splits the wood along the grain. On cedar the hand-lift is
lighter, the solution milder and the timeline longer. It is also the material where a
roof near the end of its life is most often better replaced than treated, and when
that is the honest answer we say so before quoting. The material itself is profiled on
[cedar shake roof](/surface-library/cedar-shake-roof/).

## What moves the price on a Bellingham roof?

Every moss job is priced on site, because the amount of growth is the biggest single
variable and a photo does not show it. The main factors are these:

1. How thick and widespread the moss is, and whether lichen is mixed in with it.
2. The roof's size and pitch, and how many planes, valleys and dormers it has.
3. Whether it is composition, cedar or metal.
4. Tree cover and access, since a slope above a steep drop or a conservatory takes
   longer to protect.
5. Where the property sits, because a Lake Whatcom watershed address calls for tighter
   controls.
6. Whether the gutters are cleared in the same visit or on the follow-up.

The first treatment on a roof that has been left for years is always the dearest one.
After that the job becomes upkeep: thinner growth, less hand-lifting, a quicker
treatment. A Bellingham roof kept on a regular cycle costs noticeably less per visit
than one that waits until the moss can be seen from the car, and it keeps more of its
granules along the way.

## When is the right time to treat?

Early autumn, for most Bellingham roofs. Treating in September or early October sends
the moss into the wet season already dead, and the rain that would normally feed it
carries it off instead. Spring works as well, once there are enough dry days for the
solution to sit on the growth rather than being rinsed away by the next shower.

Late summer is the worst moment to judge a roof. Moss dries to a grey crust in August
and looks like far less than it is. Take a look in the wet months, when the cushions
are swollen and bright green, and you will see the true extent.

For most houses here, treatment every three to five years keeps things in hand: nearer
three under heavy conifer cover, nearer five on an open lot. Leave the gutters for a
couple of months after a treatment, until the dead moss has come down;
[gutter cleaning](/locations/bellingham/gutter-cleaning/) is usually booked around that
timing.

## What happens to the runoff?

It stays out of the storm drains. Bellingham's stormwater code, BMC 15.42, does not
allow pressure wash water into the storm system; it has to go to sanitary sewer or a
vegetated area, and we treat what rinses off a roof with the same care as what comes
off a driveway. The complication on a roof is that the gutters gather everything and
the downspouts decide where it goes, and on many houses a downspout vanishes into a
pipe in the ground.

So before any treatment goes on, we check where each downspout discharges and plan the
rinse around it, soak the planting below the eaves, and keep the application tight to
the growth rather than flooding the whole slope.

On the east side, the Lake Whatcom watershed adds a further layer. The lake supplies
Bellingham's drinking water, and in Basin One of the watershed the City applies a
zero-phosphorus rule to fertilisers and soil amendments. That rule is written for lawns
and gardens rather than roofs, but it shows what the City is guarding, and it is why a
watershed address gets its own category on our quotes. The detail is set out at
[wash water and storm drains](/compliance/wash-water-and-storm-drains/).

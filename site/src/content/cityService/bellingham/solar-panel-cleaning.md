Solar panels in Bellingham are fighting two things at once: a short sunny season
and a lot of trees. The panels only earn their keep for part of the year, and that
part happens to be when the rain has mostly stopped and the canopy is shedding
pollen, seed and sap onto the glass.

What follows is how that plays out on local roofs, what a clean involves, and when
we would tell you to leave the array alone.

## Why doesn't the rain do the job?

Because it falls at the wrong time. The City's own figures put annual rainfall at
about 36 inches, with roughly 80% of it arriving between October and March. Those
are the months with the lowest sun and the least production, so the heavy rinsing
happens when it matters least.

From April onward the showers thin out, and the glass is left to collect whatever
the season brings. By midsummer an array under trees can be carrying months of
residue at exactly the point it should be producing the most.

Even when rain does fall on dirty panels, it tends to rearrange grime more than it
removes it. Water runs down each panel, stalls against the raised lower frame and
leaves its load there as it dries. That grey stripe along the bottom edge is a
familiar sight on arrays in a damp climate, and because cells are wired in series,
a narrow band can drag down far more output than its size suggests. The
[solar output dropped](/services/solar-panel-cleaning/solar-output-dropped/) page
works through that effect in more detail.

## What lands on panels under Bellingham trees?

The canopy covers about 40% of the city, and the species mix determines what ends
up on the glass:

- **Douglas-fir and western redcedar** drop needles that lodge between panels and
  under the frames, and in spring they release a yellow-green pollen film that
  rain smears rather than lifts
- **Big-leaf maple** sheds large leaves that mat on low-tilt arrays and hold water
  against the glass for days
- **Black cottonwood and red alder** grow quickly and can reach over a roof within
  a few years of a system going in
- **Street trees**, which the City lists as mostly maples, cherries and oaks, add
  leaves, blossom and sap wherever a house sits along a planted street

Birds are the other regular. A dropping over a single cell behaves like a patch of
shade, and it does not rinse off in a shower.

Bellingham's wind is southerly for eleven months of the year. It is worth
glancing at what stands on the south side of your lot, because most of what the
wind carries off those trees is heading toward your roof.

## Which Bellingham homes see the most soiling?

It follows the trees more than the neighborhood, but there are patterns.

The newer construction in the north and east of the city, around Cordata, Barkley
and Irongate, carries a good share of flush-mounted solar. Flush mounting means the
panels sit at whatever pitch the roof has, and on a shallow roof that means slow
runoff and a heavier edge band.

The wooded south end, from Edgemoor through Fairhaven and South Hill toward
Chuckanut, puts more arrays under mature conifer and cedar. There the issue is
needles in the gaps and moss starting at the frames, where shade and damp never
really let up.

Arrays on open ground with steep, south-facing roofs are the ones that most often
do fine for years with nothing but rain, and we will say so if that is yours.

## What does a solar clean include?

A standard visit covers:

1. Looking at the array, the mounting, the pitch and the access before anything
   gets wet, and noting any trees overhanging it
2. Checking your monitoring with you where it is available, so there is a
   before-and-after reference
3. Clearing needles and leaves from the gaps between panels and along the frames
4. Washing every panel with purified water and a soft brush on a pole
5. Working the bottom frame edge of each panel specifically, since that is where
   most of the loss sits
6. Removing moss and growth at the frames and around the array where it can be
   reached safely
7. Photographing anything that looks wrong, such as cracked glass, browning cells or
   loose cabling, for you to pass to your installer

If the roof under the array is mossy, that is usually worth addressing at the same
time; our [Bellingham roof cleaning](/locations/bellingham/roof-cleaning/) page
covers that side.

## How is the glass cleaned without damaging it?

Purified water, soft bristles, low pressure, and nobody standing on the array.

The glass on a modern panel carries an anti-reflective coating that lets more
light through to the cells. It is thin, and it is easily hurt. Scouring pads and
stiff brushes leave fine scratches that scatter light for the rest of the panel's
life. Household detergents leave a slightly sticky film that grabs the next round
of dust. Tap water dries into mineral spots. Purified water avoids all three: it
lifts the grime, rinses clean and dries without leaving anything behind.

Pressure stays low because the edge seals matter. Force water past a seal and it
can reach the laminate, which is how panels fail early.

And we never walk on the panels. Weight on the glass can crack the silicon cells
underneath without any visible sign, and the damage shows up years later as output
that falls faster than it should. Most local arrays can be reached from the ground
or from ladders with extension poles. Where roof access is needed, the crew stays
on the roof beside the array, not on it.

## What drives the cost of cleaning an array here?

We quote after seeing the system. The factors that move the figure are:

- **Panel count**, which sets the base
- **How the array is reached**: from the ground, by ladder, or from the roof
- **Roof pitch and where the array sits** on it
- **Condition**, since a first clean with moss established at the frames takes far
  longer than a yearly maintenance wash
- **Debris and growth** under or around the array that needs clearing
- **Other work on the same visit**, which usually brings the per-item cost down
  because setup is shared

There is no price printed here; we would rather see the array than guess at it.

## When should a Bellingham array be cleaned?

**Late spring** earns its keep most. Once the conifer pollen stops falling, a clean
sets the glass up for the long days of June and July, when every clear hour counts.
Clean before the pollen is finished and you will be cleaning again.

**Early autumn** is the second option, particularly for arrays under maples and
street trees, before leaf fall and before the rain returns.

**Cool conditions** matter on the day. Cold water on sun-heated glass is a thermal
shock risk, so we work mornings, evenings or overcast days, which in Bellingham is
seldom hard to arrange.

**Not every year for everyone.** If your monitoring shows steady output against the
same month last year, the array is probably fine. A sharp, sudden drop is more
likely a fault than dirt, and that is a call for your installer.

## Where does the water go afterwards?

Onto the lawn or the planting, not down a drain. Bellingham Municipal Code 15.42
bars pressure wash water from entering the storm system and requires it to go to
sanitary sewer or a vegetated area instead. Because solar work uses purified water
and no cleaning chemicals, the runoff is about as benign as wash water gets, but we
still direct it away from inlets as a matter of habit.

On the east side, the Lake Whatcom watershed feeds the city's drinking water, and
Basin One carries its own rules on top of the citywide ones. A soap-free, chemical-free
panel clean suits that setting well. The wider picture is on our
[wash water and storm drains](/compliance/wash-water-and-storm-drains/) page.

## What problems tend to travel with a dirty array?

A few, and most start with the roof around it.

**Moss under and beside the panels.** The gap between an array and the roof is
shaded and damp all year, which suits moss. Growth that starts on the shingles can
creep to the frames. Our [Bellingham moss removal](/locations/bellingham/moss-removal/)
page covers the roof side of that.

**Needle build-up in the gaps.** It holds water, feeds growth and can block the
drainage paths that let the panels shed rain.

**Nesting.** Birds and rodents like the sheltered space beneath an array. We report
what we see; screening the perimeter is a job for your installer.

**Output loss that is not dirt.** If the glass comes up clean and the numbers do not
recover, the problem is electrical, and we will say so plainly.

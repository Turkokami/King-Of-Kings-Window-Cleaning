The shaded wall of an Everson house tells you most of what you need to know
about the valley. It goes green first, it stays wet longest, and by the end of
a wet winter it can look years older than the sunny side of the same building.

What follows is how we wash houses in town: what is actually growing on the
siding, why low pressure is the only sensible choice, how the mix changes with
the age of the house, and what we do about rinse water.

## What grows on Everson siding, and why?

Mostly algae, then mildew, then moss where things stay wettest. None of it is
dirt in the usual sense. It is living growth, and it needs two things: moisture
and time. The Nooksack valley supplies plenty of both.

For a sense of the moisture, the WRCC's regional Clearbrook cooperative
station, which is not in Everson, records an average of 46.71 inches of
precipitation a year. On a house, the effect is a wall that is damp from the
first autumn storms until spring, with only short dry spells between.

Farming adds a second layer. The land around Everson and Nooksack grows
berries, carrots, potatoes and specialty produce, and carries dairy herds.
Field work in the dry months puts fine soil into the air, and on a wall that
settles as a dull film. When the rain comes back, that film holds moisture
against the siding and gives algae an easier start.

## Which parts of the house get it worst?

The pattern is predictable enough that we can usually guess it from the
driveway:

- **North-facing walls**, which get little or no direct sun in winter
- **Walls behind shrubs or hedges**, where air barely moves
- **The lower courses**, which catch splash-back from soil and gravel
- **Under a leaking or overflowing gutter**, where water runs down the face
- **Soffits and eave corners**, which collect spider webs and stay shaded
- **Sides facing a neighbour's house** a few metres away

If the north side of your house is the problem, our page on
[north-facing wall mildew](/services/house-washing/north-facing-wall-mildew/)
goes into why it keeps coming back and what slows it down.

## What is a soft wash, and why not a pressure washer?

Soft washing means low pressure, roughly what a garden hose delivers, with a
cleaning mix that kills the growth and a thorough rinse afterwards. The mix does
the work. The water just carries it on and takes it away.

A pressure washer does the opposite. It relies on force, and siding is not built
for force. Lap siding sheds water running down it; a jet aimed at it drives water
up and behind the laps, around window trim and into the wall. On older vinyl it
can crack panels, on painted wood it strips paint, and on fibre cement it can
wear the surface. The growth usually comes back faster too, because blasting
removes the visible part and leaves the roots.

Our [house washing](/services/house-washing/) page goes deeper into the method
and the chemistry. The short version for an Everson homeowner is that a proper
wash looks gentle, takes a bit longer, and lasts.

## What does an Everson house wash include?

A standard wash covers the whole exterior shell above the foundation:

1. **Pre-wetting beds and shrubs** near the walls
2. **Soft washing every elevation** of siding, working bottom to top
3. **Soffits, eaves and fascia**, including webs and wasp nests
4. **Gutter faces**, the visible outside of the run
5. **Trim, doors and window frames**, with glass rinsed so no mix dries on it
6. **Final rinse of the house and the planting** before we leave

Decks, patios, fences and outbuildings are quoted separately, since the
surfaces and the methods vary. Glass cleaning with screens and tracks is a
separate job as well; plenty of people book the
[Everson window clean](/locations/everson/window-cleaning/) straight after the
wash so both are done on the same day.

## How does the age of the house change the method?

A great deal, and Everson's housing covers a wide spread of ages. The town has 105 homes
built in 1939 or earlier and 77 built since 2020, with the big 1970s and 1980s
building decades in between. That range shows up on the walls.

### Pre-war and older painted wood

Old painted boards have usually been recoated several times, and the paint can
be chalking, cracking or lifting at the edges. We use the lowest effective
strength of mix, rinse generously, and treat any loose or flaking paint as
something to point out, not something to wash off. Washing old paint hard is
how you end up needing a painter.

### 1970s and 1980s homes

These two decades are the largest group in town, and houses of that era can carry almost anything: original or replacement vinyl, hardboard, painted panel siding or cedar. Vinyl from this era can
be brittle after decades of sun, which is one more reason to keep pressure low.
For vinyl specifics, the [vinyl siding](/surface-library/vinyl-siding/) profile
covers what it tolerates and what it does not.

### Newer construction

Fibre cement and newer vinyl clean easily, but a new house often has
construction dust, mortar splash or overspray on it that nobody has dealt with.
We handle that gently on the first visit and keep an eye out for anything that
looks like a warranty issue, which we would rather flag than touch.

### Manufactured homes

Everson has 75 mobile homes, and they get the same low-pressure approach with a
few extra checks: skirting, underfloor vents and thinner trim panels all need
care so water goes down and away, not in and under.

## What about the silt line after high water?

Where a property has taken floodwater, it usually leaves a visible tide mark
of silt on siding, foundations and fences. The Nooksack is closely monitored
here, and the National Weather Service's flood-impact statement for its Everson
overflow gauge says the water reaches low-lying areas around the City of
Nooksack as well as crossing SR 544.

We do not assume any particular house floods, and we will not guess about
yours. If you have a silt line, we can usually lift it with the same soft wash
approach once the ground is firm enough to work on. The sooner it is dealt
with after the water drops, the easier it comes off.

## Can you wash a rental or a small multi-unit building?

Yes, and a fair share of Everson's homes fall into that category. Of 988
occupied homes in town, 369 are rented, and 110 housing units sit in buildings
of three or four units. Landlords and property managers tend to want the same
things from us: a clean exterior between tenancies, a job that does not disturb
the people living there, and a clear note of anything that needs a repair.

For a multi-unit building we agree the access in advance, so tenants know which
day to move cars, close windows and bring in anything left against the walls.
We work one elevation at a time so nobody is shut out of an entrance for long,
and we report back on what we saw: failed caulking, a cracked vent cover, a
gutter spilling onto a walkway. None of that is part of the wash, but a
property owner who lives elsewhere usually wants to hear about it.

Rented single-family houses are straightforward. We only need the owner's
go-ahead, a heads-up to the tenant and a working outdoor tap.

## What drives the cost of washing an Everson house?

Prices are set on site, but these are the things that move the number:

- **Size and number of storeys**, since taller walls need more setup
- **Siding type**, with older painted wood taking more care and time
- **How much growth is on it**, and how long it has been there
- **Access**, including slopes, fences, hedges and outbuildings close to the walls
- **Extras** such as decks, fences, a detached garage or window cleaning

Returning customers generally pay less per visit than first-timers, because a
house kept on a regular cycle never builds up the same load of growth.

## When should an Everson house be washed?

Late spring through early autumn, on a dry day. The siding needs to be dry
enough for the mix to work and warm enough that it does not freeze on the wall.

Many people in the valley like to wash in late spring, once winter growth has
had its season, then follow with gutters in autumn before the heavy rain
returns. Others wait until the end of the dry months so any field dust from
summer comes off at the same time. Either works. What we avoid is washing in a
cold snap, or on a day when rain is forecast before the rinse can finish.

## Where does the wash water go?

Onto lawn and planting, where it can soak in, and away from any storm drain.
We keep detergent strength to what the surface actually requires and rinse
thoroughly, which dilutes what little is left.

statement about its specific rules on wash water. Rules do differ between
jurisdictions around the county. Our
[wash water and storm drains](/compliance/wash-water-and-storm-drains/) page

which is one more reason we treat what goes on the ground with respect.

The green that creeps up the bottom of Lynden siding is not dirt. It is living
growth, algae and mildew for the most part, and it comes back faster than people
expect on the walls that stay damp longest. Rinsing it off with a hose does
almost nothing. Blasting it with a pressure washer takes it off along with some of
the finish, and pushes water somewhere you do not want it.

What works is a soft wash: a cleaning solution that kills and loosens the growth,
applied at low pressure and rinsed off gently. The complication in this town is
the rinse, because Lynden's code has an opinion about where it may go.

## What grows on Lynden siding, and where?

Mostly green organic growth, and mostly in two places. The first is the lower
courses of each wall, the first few boards above the ground, where rain splashes
back off beds, lawn and paths and keeps the siding wet long after the rest of the
wall has dried. The second is any side of the house that gets little or no direct
sun, whether a north wall, a wall shaded by a neighbour's house, or one tucked
behind a hedge.

Both have the same cause: time spent wet. The Clearbrook cooperative station, the
nearest long-record weather station to Lynden, averages 46.71 inches of
precipitation a year, most of it in the autumn and winter. A wall that never
properly dries between October and spring gives growth months of uninterrupted
damp to settle in. The pattern on shaded elevations specifically is covered on our
[north-facing wall mildew page](/services/house-washing/north-facing-wall-mildew/).

## Why soft wash rather than pressure wash?

Because the growth is alive and rooted into the surface texture, and pressure
alone does not kill it. A high-pressure jet strips what it can reach and leaves
the rest to regrow, while also doing things to the house you would rather it did
not:

- It can force water up and behind lapped siding, into the wall cavity
- It can scar or fur the surface of painted wood and strip weakened paint
- It can crack or dislodge older vinyl and loosen trim
- It can drive water past window seals and into vents
- It sends a fine spray of loosened growth across windows and neighbouring surfaces

A soft wash reaches the whole surface at a pressure closer to a garden hose, gives
the solution a few minutes to work, and then rinses. On vinyl, the most forgiving
common siding, it is still the right approach, for reasons covered in the
[vinyl siding profile](/surface-library/vinyl-siding/).

## What is included in a house wash?

The standard wash covers every accessible wall of the house:

1. Beds, shrubs and lawn along the walls pre-wetted before any solution goes on
2. Vents, outlets and light fittings checked and protected where needed
3. Solution applied from the bottom of each wall up, so it does not streak
4. Dwell time for the solution to break down the growth
5. A low-pressure rinse from the top down, with planting rinsed again
6. Soffits, fascia and the outside of the gutters included where they are washable
7. A walk round with you at the end, in daylight

Decks, driveways, fences and outbuildings are quoted separately. Those black
streaks on the outer face of the gutters come off with a dedicated treatment
called [gutter brightening](/services/gutter-cleaning/gutter-brightening/), which
we can add to the same visit.

## What does Lynden's code say about wash water?

It says two things that need to be read together. Section 13.24.160(C)(4) of the
Lynden Municipal Code lists routine external building wash-down as a conditional
discharge to the storm system, permitted only when no detergents are used. Section
13.24.160(A), meanwhile, lists illicit discharges, and item 15 on that list is
soaps, detergents or ammonia. Item 14 is steam-cleaning wastes.

The practical reading is simple. Plain water rinsed off a building is tolerated.
Water carrying a cleaning solution is not allowed into the storm system at all.
And the city's own stormwater page asks residents to clean up debris before and
after pressure washing, which applies equally to what a wash loosens from a wall.

## How do you keep the rinse out of the storm drains?

By deciding where it goes before the first wall gets wet. Most of what enters
Lynden's storm drains flows to Fishtrap Creek and then the Nooksack River, so a
wash that runs across a driveway and into the gutter at the kerb is not just
untidy; it breaks the rule above.

On the quote, we look at which way each wall drains. Where lawn or planting sits
below the siding, the rinse goes into the ground, which is where it belongs.
Where a wall stands over paving that slopes to the street, we change how that
section is washed and rinsed so the water is taken up before it reaches a drain,
and where that is genuinely not achievable, we tell you on the quote instead of
finding out on the day. Loosened growth and grit get swept up afterward. Our [wash
water and storm drain page](/compliance/wash-water-and-storm-drains/) describes
the approach across all our work.

## Does an older Lynden house need a different wash?

Often it does. Lynden's housing is mostly young, with about 45 percent of its
6,210 units built in 2000 or later, but the census still counts 483 homes from
1939 or earlier and 410 from the 1950s. Those older houses are more likely to
carry painted wood, several generations of paint, or original trim, and every one
of those takes a gentler wash than a factory-finished panel.

On an older house we test a small area first, use a milder mix, and pay closer
attention to paint that is chalking or lifting. Washing cannot restore a failing
finish, and a firm spray can make it worse, so if paint is coming away we point it
out before we start rather than after.

## When is the right time to wash a Lynden house?

The dry end of the year. Nearby records show July as the driest month by a wide
margin, and a wash done between late spring and early autumn dries properly,
lets us judge the result in good light, and gives the siding a clean start before
the wet season. Washing in freezing weather is off the table, because the
solution does not work well and the rinse can ice on paths.

## How can I slow the growth coming back?

Mostly by giving the wall a chance to dry. The growth we removed in August 2026
had settled on the lower courses and a shaded wall, the spots siding stays wet longest, and the same logic works
in reverse: shorten the wet spells and regrowth slows.

A few changes make a real difference on most Lynden houses:

- Trim shrubs and hedges back so there is a hand's width or more of air between
  planting and siding
- Keep bark and soil in beds a little below the bottom board, so splash has
  further to travel
- Point sprinkler heads away from the walls, since summer irrigation keeps the
  lower courses damp in the one season they should be drying
- Make sure the gutters are carrying water away rather than spilling it down the
  face of the wall, which our [Lynden gutter page](/locations/lynden/gutter-cleaning/)
  covers in detail
- Rinse the bottom of a shaded wall with plain water now and then through the
  summer, before new growth gets a foothold

None of this replaces a wash on a wall that has already gone green. It does
stretch the time between washes, sometimes considerably, and it costs nothing.

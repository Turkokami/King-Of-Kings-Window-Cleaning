A roof in Lynden spends most of the year wet and a short part of it dry, and that
split is the whole story of what grows on it. Moss, algae and lichen settle in
through the long damp season, hold water against the roofing through the
coldest months, and look their worst by early spring.

The fix is not force. We treat the roof with a low-pressure soft wash, take heavy
moss off by hand, and let the weather finish the rinse over the weeks that
follow. The timing of all that is set by the local rainfall, which has a very
clear shape.

## What grows on a Lynden roof?

Three things, usually in combination. Moss forms the green cushions that sit in
the gaps between shingles and along the lower edge of each course. Algae shows up
as dark streaks running down the slope, often mistaken for dirt or ageing. Lichen
appears as crusty grey-green spots that grip the surface harder than either of
the others.

All three want the same conditions: a surface that stays damp for long spells and
does not get much sun. That makes the shaded slope of any roof the first to go,
followed by the areas under overhanging branches, along valleys where water
concentrates, and on the lower courses where runoff from the whole slope passes
over. An open slope in full sun can go years with little growth, while the other
side of the same ridge carries a thick coat.

Heavy moss does more than look untidy. Its roots work into the joints between
shingles and lift the edges, which lets wind-driven rain in. The mechanics of that
are laid out on our [moss under shingles page](/services/moss-removal/moss-under-shingles/).

## How does the Lynden rain calendar affect the roof?

It decides when treatment will actually work. The long-record station nearest
Lynden is the Western Regional Climate Center's cooperative station at Clearbrook,
with a record running from 1903 to 2016 and an annual average of 46.71 inches of
precipitation. It is in the area rather than in town, so take its figures as the
local pattern rather than a reading from your street.

Month by month, the contrast is stark. November averages 6.17 inches and December
6.22, while July averages only 1.47. The same station records an average of 16
inches of snow a year, most of it falling in December and January.

For a roof, that sequence means a treatment applied in the dry part of summer gets
a long run of dry days to work, and then the autumn rain does the rinsing. Growth
left untreated goes into November already established, spends the wettest two
months of the year soaking up water, and then sits under snow and ice through the
coldest ones.

## How do you clean a roof without damaging it?

By using chemistry and patience instead of pressure. The steps, on a typical
Lynden roof, run like this:

1. Planting below the eaves pre-wetted and, where needed, covered
2. Downspouts checked so we know where the runoff will go
3. Thick moss removed by hand, working down the slope with the grain of the
   roofing so nothing is lifted or scraped
4. A cleaning solution applied at low pressure across the affected areas
5. Planting rinsed again once the application is finished
6. Loose material cleared from the gutters at the end of the visit

The solution kills the remaining growth and loosens the staining, and the rain
over the following weeks carries it away. That is why a soft-washed roof looks
better a month after the visit than on the afternoon itself.

What we do not do is put a pressure washer on a roof. On composition shingle it
strips the mineral granules that protect the asphalt from sunlight, and those
granules do not grow back. On cedar it can split and fray the wood. On any roof it
drives water up under the laps. Our library pages go into how each surface
behaves: [composition asphalt shingle](/surface-library/composition-asphalt-shingle/),
[cedar shake](/surface-library/cedar-shake-roof/) and [standing seam
metal](/surface-library/standing-seam-metal-roof/).

## Does the age of the roof change the approach?

It changes how carefully we work, more than what we use. Lynden's housing is
young by regional standards, with a median year built of 1997, so many roofs in
town are on their first or second covering. A newer roof with a light coat of
algae is a quick, straightforward treatment.

An older or heavily grown roof takes more time on the hand removal and more
caution on the footing. Brittle shingles and weathered cedar get lighter handling,
and anywhere the roof feels soft or looks lifted we stop walking it. Where a roof
is simply at the end of its life, cleaning will make it look tidier, but it will
not add years, and we would rather tell you that than take the job.

## What does Lynden's code say about roof runoff?

It says cleaning chemistry must not reach the storm system. Chapter 13.24 of the
Lynden Municipal Code lists soaps, detergents or ammonia as illicit discharges to
the city storm system (item 15), and the only building wash-down it tolerates is
the kind that uses no detergent at all. The city's stormwater page notes that most
of what enters its drains ends up in Fishtrap Creek and then the Nooksack River.

A roof sheds everything into its gutters and downspouts, so the question is where
each downspout goes. Where a drop discharges onto lawn or into a bed, runoff is
taken up by the ground. Where it runs into a pipe that ties to the street system,
we plan around it before we start, and apply only what the growth needs rather
than drenching the slope. If a downspout's destination cannot be managed, it goes
on the quote as something to discuss.

## What sets the price of a Lynden roof clean?

It is quoted on site after looking at the roof, and it depends on:

- **Roof area**, and how much of it actually has growth
- **Pitch**, since a steep roof needs more rigging and slower movement
- **Height**, especially on two-storey houses and those with dormers
- **Roofing material**, with cedar and older shingle taking the most care
- **Amount of moss**, since thick mats take far longer to remove by hand than a
  film of algae takes to treat
- **Access around the house**, including decks, conservatories and planting
- **What else is booked**, as gutters and windows cost less added to the same trip

## What should I expect after the visit?

A roof that is clearly better on the day and noticeably better a few weeks later.
Heavy moss will be gone by the time we pack up. Dark algae streaks fade over the
following weeks as rain rinses the loosened growth away. Lichen is the slowest to
shift and sometimes leaves a pale mark where it gripped the surface.

For a while, bits of dead moss will wash down into the gutters. That is expected,
and it is one reason we suggest having the troughs cleared at the end of the job
or after the first autumn rains. The [Lynden gutter
page](/locations/lynden/gutter-cleaning/) covers timing for that.

## What can I do to keep the roof clean longer?

Most of it comes down to sun and air. Branches that shade the roof or rest on it
keep the surface damp and drop debris that holds water, so cutting them back
buys years between treatments. Keeping gutters clear stops water backing up
under the lowest course, where moss often starts. And looking up once a year,
ideally in early spring when growth is most visible, catches a problem while it
is still a light treatment rather than a heavy removal.

## Is a snowy winter hard on a mossy roof?

Harder than on a clean one. Moss behaves like a sponge, so a roof carrying a thick
coat goes into December holding water in every cushion. When the temperature drops,
that water freezes in place against the shingles and in the joints between them,
and a freeze-thaw cycle repeated through a winter works those joints open a little
further each time.

Snow adds to it. With 16 inches a year on average at Clearbrook, much of it in
December and January, a Lynden roof can spend stretches of midwinter under a
blanket that melts from underneath. Meltwater running down under the snow meets
moss dams along the lower courses and at the gutter line, backs up, and looks for
the nearest gap. A clean roof sheds that water; a mossy one holds it.

None of that means a mossy roof is about to fail. It means the case for treating
growth in summer, before the wet and the cold, is stronger here than a look from
the driveway suggests. The general picture of how and why we clean roofs this way
is on our main [roof cleaning service page](/services/roof-cleaning/).

## What we will not claim

We clean roofs; we do not certify them. Nobody on our crew is a roof inspector or
an engineer, and a clean is not an assessment of the roof's condition. If we see
something that worries us, we tell you what it was and where, and suggest a
roofer. We will not tell you a roof is sound, and we will not sell you a cleaning
as a substitute for a repair.

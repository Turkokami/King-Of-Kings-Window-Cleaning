Most exterior cleaning companies in this region do not mention the stormwater
codes at all, and on a lot of jobs it genuinely does not come up. It comes up on
the ones where water runs somewhere it should not, and by then the conversation is
with a municipality rather than with a contractor.

This page sets out what the two codes we work under most often actually say, with
the sections cited so you can read them yourself.

## The short version

**Ferndale has an explicit allowance.** FMC 13.35 is titled *Water Quality –
Prohibited Discharges*, and subsection 13.35.020(B) lists allowable discharges.
Item 4 covers "street and sidewalk wash water, water used to control dust, and
routine external building wash down that does not use detergents." That is a
deliberate carve-out for exactly the kind of plain-water rinse an exterior
cleaner does.

**Bellingham has no equivalent.** BMC 15.42 is the city's stormwater management
chapter, and 15.42.050(C)(2) prohibits any person from discharging — or allowing
others under their control to discharge — into the stormwater drainage system or
to surface and ground waters "any materials other than stormwater." There is no
allowable-discharge list in that section to fall back on.

Ten miles separate the two, and the compliant job is not the same job in both
places.

## What this changes on site

The practical consequence is about containment, not permission.

On a **Ferndale** property, a straightforward detergent-free rinse — a plain-water
wash down of siding or hardscape — sits inside a written allowance. That is a
genuinely useful thing to know, and it is worth confirming that what is planned
actually is detergent-free, because the allowance ends the moment a cleaning
solution enters the picture.

On a **Bellingham** property, and on any job anywhere involving chemistry, the
work is planned around not discharging. In practice that means:

- **Inlets in the work zone covered or bermed** before anything is applied.
- **Rinse directed to vegetation** rather than to a drain, so it filters through
  soil instead of entering the system.
- **Planting pre-wetted and rinsed**, which both protects the plants and stops
  anything concentrating in one spot.
- **Recovery rather than release** where the job and the surface allow it.
- **The sloping of the site read before starting**, because a driveway that falls
  toward the street is a different problem from one that falls toward a lawn.

None of that is exotic and none of it is slow. It is the difference between a
crew that has thought about where the water goes and a crew that has not.

## The Lake Whatcom watershed

Properties in the Lake Whatcom watershed — which includes Sudden Valley and a
substantial part of the eastern side of Bellingham — carry protection beyond the
city's general stormwater provisions, because the lake is a drinking water source.

We treat watershed addresses as their own category when quoting rather than as
ordinary Bellingham jobs. If a property is inside it, that is worth saying at the
quote stage, because it shapes the method rather than the price.

## Jurisdiction is not obvious from the address

This causes more confusion than the codes themselves.

A Ferndale mailing address does not establish that a property is inside Ferndale
city limits. A great deal of what people describe as "Ferndale" or "Bellingham" is
unincorporated Whatcom County, which is a separate regulatory regime again, and
the boundaries are irregular around the edges of both cities.

Establishing which jurisdiction a property is actually in is part of quoting
anything that produces runoff, and it is a question worth asking of any contractor
who does not raise it themselves.

## Who the rules apply to, and what they require

| Where the property is | What the code requires | What it means on a job |
|---|---|---|
| Ferndale city limits | FMC 13.35.020(B)(4) allows "routine external building wash down that does not use detergents" | A plain-water rinse sits inside a written allowance. Add a cleaning solution and it does not. |
| Ferndale, any job using chemistry | No allowance applies — the general prohibition governs | Containment, inlets covered, rinse directed to vegetation. |
| Bellingham city limits | BMC 15.42.050(C)(2) prohibits discharging anything other than stormwater to the system or to surface and ground water | No carve-out for wash down at all. Every job is planned around not discharging. |
| Lake Whatcom watershed | BMC 16.80 reservoir provisions apply on top of 15.42, targeting phosphorus under an Ecology TMDL response plan | Tighter again. Treated as its own quote category rather than an ordinary address. |
| Unincorporated Whatcom or Skagit County | Neither city code applies; county and state rules do | Jurisdiction established before quoting, because a mailing address is not a city limit. |

Penalties are set by each jurisdiction's enforcement provisions rather than by
this page, and enforcement in practice is complaint-driven. The liability is
worth knowing regardless: it attaches to the property as well as to the
contractor, so an illicit discharge at your address remains your problem after
the crew has gone.

## Why a cleaning contractor's chemistry is a stormwater question

The codes above talk about discharge, not about products, so it is worth
connecting the two.

Exterior cleaning in this region is mostly chemical rather than mechanical — that
is the whole argument against pressure on a roof, on siding, on cedar. The
solutions that do the work are effective precisely because they are biologically
active, and the same property that kills moss on a north slope is the property
that makes a release into surface water a real environmental event rather than a
paperwork one.

Three things follow.

**The rinse matters more than the application.** What goes onto a roof is
controlled and targeted. What comes off it, twenty minutes later, carries
everything it dissolved and travels wherever the site slopes. The plan for the
rinse is the compliance plan.

**Dilution is not a defence, but volume genuinely changes the risk.** A
concentrated release at one point is a different event from a rinse spread across
a lawn that filters through soil. Directing the water is the single most effective
control available on a residential job.

**Vegetation is the intended destination, and it needs preparing.** Soil filters;
a storm drain does not. That is why planting below a work area is pre-wetted
before anything is applied and rinsed afterwards — a plant already saturated with
clean water absorbs far less of whatever lands on it, and a lawn receiving a
spread rinse handles it in a way a drain inlet never will.

## Commercial and multi-unit sites raise the stakes

Everything above scales badly in the direction you would expect.

A commercial property or an apartment block has more hard surface, more inlets,
and less soil for water to disappear into. Parking areas drain to the system by
design. A site that was engineered to move water off it quickly is, by
construction, a site with fewer places for a rinse to go.

Commercial work also tends to carry its own overlay: a property manager's
requirements, an HOA's rules, a tenant's lease obligations, and on some sites an
industrial stormwater permit with conditions of its own. Those are contractual and
regulatory layers sitting on top of the municipal code, not instead of it.

For scheduled commercial contracts this is settled once, at the start, rather than
negotiated per visit — which is one of the practical arguments for a contract over
a series of one-off calls.

## What this page is and is not

It is a plain-language summary of two specific code sections as they read on the
review date at the top of this page, written so a property owner can understand
what governs work on their building and check it themselves. Every source is
linked.

It is not legal advice, and it is not a substitute for reading the code or asking
the municipality. Municipal codes are amended, section numbering changes, and a
summary written in September is not automatically true in March. If a decision
turns on this, read the current text at the links above or call the city.

What it is meant to demonstrate is simpler: that the question has been asked. An
exterior cleaning contractor working across two counties and a dozen
jurisdictions either knows which rules apply where, or is discovering it on your
property.
